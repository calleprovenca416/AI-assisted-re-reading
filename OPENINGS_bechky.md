# Openings of the readings of Bechky, B. A. 2020. Evaluative spillovers from technological change: The effects of "DNA envy" on occupational practices in forensic science. Administrative Science Quarterly, 65(3): 606–643.

The readings of this paper, in their order, each followed by its opening: two sentences another reader wrote, the first on what the reading opens in the paper's argument, the second on the next investigation that would settle it.

## Reading R01

**The reading.** Narcotics analysts' resistance could primarily be a defense of a workable production system under legal deadlines and limited equipment. A technique can be available and scientifically acceptable without being feasible for the entire caseload. Read this way, the contrast with toxicology reflects the unequal costs and scale of the requested changes: toxicology added calculations to established work, while narcotics faced replacing its main route through a heavy workload. Occupational preferences would reinforce this difference without necessarily causing it. Narcotics analysts already used GC/MS for unfamiliar substances or drugs unsuitable for crystal testing (p. 629). They nevertheless relied on rapid crystal tests for common cases because local law imposed short turnaround times. This distinguishes resistance to universal deployment from rejection of instrumentation itself. Analysts contested delays introduced by a new reporting system and discussed differences measured in minutes (p. 631). This shows that small processing costs mattered in everyday work even when DNA comparisons were not the immediate issue. An analyst explained that two available instruments and lengthy runs could not accommodate the caseload (p. 632). The proposed second examination of crystals would require repeating the tests, while microscope cameras were discussed as a less expensive alternative to more GC/MS instruments. These passages identify concrete capacity constraints and possible remedies. Toxicology's revised reporting drew on established equipment, methods, and accumulated data (p. 628). This supplies a comparison in which the change could build on existing capacity rather than displace the main production method.

**Its opening.** If narcotics kept crystal tests because two slow instruments could not clear its caseload under statutory deadlines, the contrast with toxicology reflects unequal capacity and displacement costs rather than opposing occupational values. Comparing narcotics units in laboratories with ample chromatography instruments or looser statutory turnaround times would show whether analysts still defend crystal testing when throughput constraints are removed.

## Reading R02

**The reading.** Compatibility is settled inside one laboratory, one facet at a time, not between a method and DNA profiling. The pathway picks the facet—error measurement, instrument output, population statistics—and the lab's instruments, records, protocols and statutes decide whether it can be met at scale. Toxicology adapted because its quality-control history, traceability log and mandated confidence interval had already paid for the change. Narcotics held a compatible method, but two instruments, long runs and a statutory charging clock kept it off routine cases. Firearms, already imaging their striae, were incompatible only on the facet the critics chose. Feasibility is where a method meets a budget and a clock. DNA profiling meant something different to each group: an overarching statistical practice for firearms, error measurement for toxicology, the certainty of instrument output for narcotics (p. 634). Compatibility was tested not against the whole benchmark but against the facet each pathway made salient, which is the hinge of the reading. GC/MS's logs and graphs were seen in the narcotics community as matching DNA standards, yet at MCCL crystal tests carried most cases because the jurisdiction's law demanded rapid turnaround for charging and methamphetamine could be confirmed by crystals in minutes (pp. 628–629). Establishes both the compatible method and the statutory clock under which it had to perform. Robin says the lab could not keep up with its caseload without crystals, having two instruments with long runs; the unit notes a second-analyst review would double analysis time and weighs microscope cameras as far cheaper than more GC/MS instruments (p. 632). Instrument count, caseload and budget are the decisive terms, and the unit pursued adaptation along the facet it could afford. Toxicology already had an ISO plan for uncertainty of measurement, a protocol logging instruments and standards, a state-mandated confidence interval, and five years of quality-control data from an unchanged instrument, from which Jason assembled the new figure (pp. 627–628). Its compatibility was a stock of prior mandates, routines and records that had absorbed most of the cost before the challenge arrived. Examiners build note packets of digital images over several days and recount that sight-based identification is no longer acceptable now that images are required (pp. 621–622). Firearms had already met an earlier objectifying demand once imaging equipment and documentation norms were in place; their incompatibility is specific to the statistical facet the critics selected.

**Its opening.** Treating compatibility as a match between a pathway-selected facet of the benchmark and a laboratory's local equipment, records, and legal deadlines shifts the explanation from occupation-wide methods to local organizational capacity. Comparing laboratories that practice the same forensic specialty under different instrument budgets, archival records, and statutory turnaround rules would test whether adaptation varies across laboratories within one occupation.

## Reading R03

**The reading.** The three responses follow what change would cost each unit in instruments, staff time and legal deadlines. Values are the language in which those costs are expressed. Toxicology changed because the change was nearly free: the data existed and the method stayed the same. Narcotics held on to crystal tests because the unit could not meet its statutory turnaround with two instruments. Firearms did nothing because nothing affordable was on offer: no population data exist for a four-person unit to report from. Local law required fast turnaround on drug cases, and the commonest drug could be confirmed by crystal test in ten to fifteen minutes (p. 629). This sets a hard throughput constraint. An analyst says the unit could not keep up with its caseload without crystals because it has only two instruments and runs are long (p. 632). The objection is stated as capacity, not preference. Second-analyst review would double analysis time, and cameras are discussed as costly but cheaper than more instruments (p. 632). The unit is pricing alternatives, which is what a cost-driven actor does. The veteran analyst's objection to instrument testing is that it takes much longer and results would not come back at once (p. 631). Speed leads her account. Analysts time themselves and fight a new data-entry system over a minute per case, which shows how tightly throughput governs the unit (p. 631). Toxicology's new reporting reused existing historical data, the same instrument and the same method, so adoption required little new resource (pp. 627–628). Other units remark that equipment, funding, grants and staff flow to the DNA unit (p. 619). The resources that would make being like DNA possible sit elsewhere. Firearms examiners say statistics are impossible because not every bullet can be examined, which is a statement about missing data infrastructure (p. 624).

**Its opening.** If each unit's response tracks what adaptation costs in instruments, staff time, data infrastructure, and legal deadlines, then occupational values express material constraints rather than independently filtering evaluative pressure. Examining whether units revise their value talk and adopt instrumented or statistical practices when given extra instruments, funding, or relaxed turnaround deadlines would test whether resource costs drive the outcomes.

## Reading R04

**The reading.** The apparent coherence of occupational values could partly be produced by people sorting themselves into work they already prefer. In particular, narcotics may collect analysts who dislike repetitive instrument work and leave units where that work predominates. Resistance would then express the preferences of a selected local membership as well as values learned through practicing an occupation. On this reading, job movement helps create the cultural differences that the paper subsequently uses to explain responses to evaluation. Several narcotics analysts had previously worked in toxicology and wanted to leave (p. 630). One described entering toxicology because it had a vacancy, then persuading management to provide training elsewhere because the balance of paperwork and benchwork was unsatisfactory. This is direct evidence of dissatisfaction preceding a move into the unit whose values it later exemplified. Criminalists entered with broadly similar educational backgrounds and were hired into particular units (p. 613). This establishes a common recruitment pool from which initial placement and subsequent movement could generate differences between unit memberships. The laboratory regarded criminalists as potentially interchangeable across units, and the director was planning a transfer involving narcotics and firearms (p. 619). This shows that the boundaries treated analytically as occupational were also crossed through organizational staffing decisions. A narcotics analyst explicitly recognized that other people preferred instrument-based routines while she preferred varied chemical work (p. 632). This permits a reading in which divergent individual preferences help determine where workers settle.

**Its opening.** If analysts who dislike routine instrument work transfer out of toxicology into narcotics, the units' contrasting cultures reflect internal personnel sorting as much as values learned from performing each task. Tracing analysts' task preferences from initial hiring through unit assignments and transfer requests would establish how much of each unit's culture comes from selection versus on-the-job socialization.

## Reading R05

**The reading.** Re-attribute what separated the two units, from fit with what each values to how much of the unit's routine the change would displace and what that would cost. On the toxicology side the change in print is to the report and the calculation behind it. Toxicology as the paper observed it was high-volume instrument work (p. 625). The protocol already logged instruments, reagents and standards, and the laboratory had been reporting at a 95% confidence level (p. 627). The courtroom attack was on reports that omitted measurement-uncertainty estimates (p. 625); the unit prepared the statistical analysis on a plan it already had (p. 627), and the new calculation used several years of existing quality-control data (pp. 627–628). No passage prints a change to how samples are prepared or run. On the narcotics side what the analysts feared losing was the routine bench method: they feared their working group might eventually prohibit crystal testing (p. 631). Crystal tests handled many routine cases and often confirmed common methamphetamine in about ten to fifteen minutes, GC/MS was reserved for unknown or unconfirmed substances, and rapid legal turnaround made the faster method especially useful (p. 629). Robin said the laboratory could not handle its caseload without crystals because it had only two relevant instruments and instrument runs took too long (p. 632). Jodi expected a shift toward slower GC/MS if crystal testing were phased out (p. 631). The analysts said a rule of independent visual confirmation would in effect require a fresh duplicate test and roughly double the work (p. 632), and they noted that microscope cameras would cost less than additional GC/MS instruments (p. 632). So the two instrument-based routes the pair holds comparable are not the same size of thing. For toxicology the instrument method was the routine and the demand added a calculation to it. For narcotics the instrument method was the exception, and what the analysts feared would have put it in place of the routine. The wager is that this difference, in how much of the day's work is displaced and what that costs in instruments and time against the caseload, is what made the units come out differently, and that the values the paper credits accompany each outcome without being shown to produce it. Two things in print lean this way. The narcotics analysts' stated reasons divide: time, work and cost in the passages on p. 631 and p. 632; variety and what the work asks of the analyst in the passages on p. 632. That they were especially troubled by the second kind is the author's account (p. 632). And on the toxicology side the participant passages show vigilance against sample-identity and pipetting mistakes (p. 626, pp. 626–627); the fit between that and estimating uncertainty across equipment, reagents and procedural steps (pp. 627–628) is made in the author's voice (p. 627; Table 2, Values — toxicology, p. 633), and the nearest participant-side item is pride shown when the new reports were presented (p. 628), which comes after the change. The reading is close to the paper in one respect: the paper's own account of narcotics values includes quick case completion (p. 630, p. 631, author's account), so speed is registered. It parts from the paper in treating instruments, run time and caseload as conditions of the laboratory, not as something the analysts value, and in crediting them for the outcome where the paper's claim (p. 628, p. 632, pp. 633–637) credits variety, autonomy and craft judgment.

**Its opening.** Because toxicology only added a calculation to its routine instrument output while narcotics faced replacing its fast everyday bench method on two slow instruments, the pair's divergence can be explained by workflow displacement and capacity cost rather than occupational values. Comparing how toxicology and narcotics respond when both face either a purely additive reporting change or a full replacement of routine bench methods would isolate value fit from the scale of workflow disruption.

## Reading R06

**The reading.** The firearms unit had already met the demand in the form comparison microscopy can carry. It replaced claims of personal recognition with an image-by-image record, countersigned by a second examiner and built to be reviewed by outsiders and to hold up on the stand; that is the reviewable-record part of the DNA model. What it refuses is the statistical part alone. Read this way, 'never been challenged' is what earlier adaptation looks like from inside, and the units differ in which piece of the DNA model each could take up, and when, more than in embracing or resisting it. Forensic biologists built their ways of presenting evidence as DNA was challenged in court; the legal parties can inspect the raw data, graphs and tables; yet the analysts regard the work behind them as messy judgment settled among colleagues (pp. 618–619). Seen from inside, the DNA model is judgment made reviewable for court, which is the part a firearms unit could take. The image record exists to back judgments on the stand; packets hold images of every compared set, matching or not; a senior examiner says outsiders have reviewed his work [6 words of Bechky (2020), p. 621]' (p. 621). The absence of local challenge can then be credited to the record. Examiners say appeals to personal recognition are no longer accepted in reports or testimony and that images are now required; a second examiner views each comparison and signs each page (p. 622). This is the change itself, with the examiners' stated reason. All three qualified examiners are trained in the counting method and one keeps it for borderline calls (p. 623). The numerical route is held in reserve. An examiner at the statewide meeting, drawing on questioning by defense attorneys, says critics build on examiners' own testimony and that it falls to examiners to be more convincing (pp. 623–624). Members of the discipline treat courtroom demand as a reason to improve the record, the motive the unit gives for its images. Toxicologists met the demand narrowly, as one standard on measurement error (p. 627). This is the paper's own precedent for a unit taking a single part of the DNA model. Narcotics analysts weigh microscope cameras and a second viewer for crystal tests, whose exposure (p. 631) is that they leave nothing to review later (p. 632). Reviewability is the DNA-like quality at stake in this lab; firearms already has it.

**Its opening.** Counting firearms examiners' earlier shift to mandatory digital images and signed peer reviews as an adaptation to the reviewable-record part of the benchmark turns firearms from a case of pure resistance into one of selective, earlier accommodation. Tracing when and why MCCL firearms examiners introduced image packets and signed technical reviews—and whether defense attorneys declined to challenge them because of those records—would test whether prior adaptation explains the absence of local court challenges.

## Reading R07

**The reading.** Re-attribute what separated the two units. The paper credits distance: remote for firearms, immediate for toxicology. A second thing varied across the pair and is printed on one side only: a change already being worked toward under a standing requirement. Before the NAS report the laboratory was adapting to ISO 17025 and the toxicology unit had a multi-year plan to estimate measurement uncertainty (p. 627). State law and ASCLD required a five-percent confidence range for blood-alcohol measurement, the protocol already logged instruments, reagents and standards, and the laboratory was reporting at a 95% confidence level while preparing fuller uncertainty estimates for ISO compliance (p. 627). When the local challenge became imminent the unit accelerated that plan and prepared the statistical analysis (p. 627). At an outreach session for criminal-justice personnel Jason rejected the idea that the laboratory had only begun uncertainty work because of the NAS report, cited ISO and NIST requirements, and said the new calculation used several years of existing quality-control data (pp. 627–628). The paper's own table puts ASCLD accreditation standards in toxicology's professional-association cell (Table 2, Professional association — toxicology, p. 633, author's account) and identifies no professional-association route for firearms (Table 2, Professional association — firearms, p. 633, author's account). On the firearms side no passage prints a plan for the unit, and none says what the laboratory's ISO 17025 adaptation (p. 627) asked of it. The one professional-body response printed on the firearms criticism is a rebuttal: AFTE leaders issued a detailed rebuttal to parts of the NAS criticism of firearms work (p. 623). IAI and AFTE also answered the report with selected rebuttals and commitments to have their research committees study the recommendations further (p. 616). One examiner's work, at least, had been under outside review: Adam says outsiders had repeatedly reviewed his work without challenging it (p. 621). Read this way, the pair does not set a unit that changed beside a unit that did not. It sets a unit whose change was already planned and required beside a unit with no plan and no requirement of its own in print, and the imminent challenge brought the plan forward. The wager is that the standing requirement is what made the difference, and that the immediate route accounts for when toxicology's reports changed, not for the fact that they did. A requirement met on a multi-year plan is hard to count among the paper's locally immediate routes (p. 606, p. 608, p. 617, p. 620, pp. 624–625, pp. 627–628, p. 632, pp. 633–636), so on this reading the pair shows the slower route doing the work. This sits close to the paper's claim (p. 625, pp. 627–628, pp. 633–637), which already lists accreditation work under way, and to the passage on p. 635, where the author's account has that work making the change easier. It parts from the paper's claim (p. 606, p. 608, p. 617, p. 620, pp. 624–625, pp. 627–628, p. 632, pp. 633–636), which takes this pair to show that immediacy supplies the impetus.

**Its opening.** If toxicology changed because an accreditation mandate had already scheduled the uncertainty calculation whereas firearms faced no comparable mandate, then the immediate local court challenge explains only the date of toxicology's reform rather than why one unit changed and the other did not. Checking what laboratory-wide accreditation standards required of firearms and whether toxicology units without a local court threat still implemented uncertainty reporting on their three-year accreditation schedule would separate accreditor mandates from courtroom immediacy.

## Reading R08

**The reading.** Retold from the forensic biology unit's own passages, the paper's episode has no event in it. None of the unit's fourteen passages has a DNA analyst speak of the 2009 report, of being the standard, or of the other units; the nearest is the supervisors introducing themselves, as a joke, on the author's first day, as the 'DNA princesses' (p. 615). What the passages do carry is the unit's account of its own work and one line of history. At the bench the analysts regard profiling as judgment-laden and untidy, because instrument output can contain artifacts and samples can be dirty, degraded or contaminated (p. 619). Terri describes a contaminated saliva control that cleared when she repeated the amplification, and Carly says contamination can persist with no identifiable cause (p. 619). In the paper's description, conclusions often took repeated interpretation and discussion between analyst and reviewer, sometimes with others brought in (p. 619), and Terri remarks that writing a report could take the wider laboratory (p. 619). What goes out is a profile released after technical and administrative review (pp. 617–618) and a report that pairs a contributor conclusion with a population-frequency figure (p. 618). And the method became established over roughly twenty-five years, after technical advances, court challenges and favorable National Academy assessments in the 1990s (p. 617, which the paper draws from cited sources). The wager from this side: the forensic biology unit is not the fixed point the other three are measured from but one more unit whose work is judgment talked through with a reviewer, and whose occupation was itself under court challenge and Academy assessment first. The firearms review in the passage on p. 622, two examiners debating how much agreement is enough, is the nearest printed counterpart to the passage on p. 619. Review of an analyst's work by someone else is printed for every unit (pp. 617–618 with p. 619, p. 622, pp. 625–626, pp. 629–630); what is printed for forensic biology alone is the population-frequency figure (p. 618). If so, what each neighbor is compared with (the paper's claim at p. 617, p. 634) and found compatible or incompatible with (the paper's claim at p. 606, p. 608, p. 617, p. 620, p. 625, pp. 627–628, p. 632, pp. 633–637; Table 2, Technique — firearms, p. 633; Table 2, Technique — toxicology, p. 633; Table 2, Technique — controlled substances (narcotics), p. 633, author's account) is what leaves the DNA unit, and the judgment that the paper's table sets against the objectivity associated with DNA (Table 2, Values — firearms, p. 633, author's account) is present on the DNA side as well (p. 619). This agrees with the paper on the substance of the paper's claim (pp. 618–619), untidy work and outputs that look objective, and uses the same passages. It differs in where it puts the unit: among the cases, with a history of being evaluated, and not above them. Whether the review steps and the figure are what that earlier evaluation left behind is not printed, and the reading does not assert it.

**Its opening.** Placing forensic biology alongside the other units as a judgment-heavy practice that previously underwent courtroom and scientific scrutiny means neighboring specialties are being measured against an outgoing report format rather than a fundamentally different kind of bench work. Historical inquiry into how forensic biology standardized its review procedures and statistical reporting during earlier court challenges and Academy reviews would show whether the benchmark unit traveled the same pathway of adaptation now confronting its neighbors.

## Reading R09

**The reading.** 'Change to practice' covers two things. X is a change that leaves in place the way the result is reached and adds to what is recorded, shown or reported with it. Y is a change that replaces the person's own looking and judging as the way the identification is reached. On the X side: firearms examiners say knowing a mark on sight is no longer acceptable and images must back the call (p. 622); they keep note packets with images of each set of striae to support judgment on the stand (p. 621); a second examiner signs each page (p. 622); toxicology adds an uncertainty range with the same instrument and method (p. 627, p. 628); narcotics analysts talk over buying microscope cameras (p. 632). On the Y side: counting striae is not regularly used (p. 623), numbers are declined (p. 624), and a move to GC/MS is feared or refused (p. 631, p. 632). Read this way the three units look alike: each has taken on or entertained an X change, none is printed taking on a Y change, and toxicology is not printed as being asked for one. The contrast between embracing and resisting would then lie partly in which kind of change each unit was presented with. This sits close to the paper's values account for the Y passages; it departs from the paper on firearms, where an X change is printed as already made.

**Its opening.** Splitting practice change into additive reporting documentation versus replacing bench judgment makes the three units look consistent—all accepted or considered additive documentation while none accepted replacing how analysts reach conclusions, and toxicology was only asked for an additive change. Examining how toxicologists respond when asked to replace an established analytic judgment and how narcotics analysts respond when asked only to attach camera images to crystal tests would test whether the type of change demanded explains the outcome.

## Reading R10

The units' different responses would then track who is in them.

**Its opening.** If performing toxicology's routine drove variety-seeking analysts out into narcotics rather than instilling a shared love of error-free repetition, then unit values record who stayed and who left rather than what the technique teaches. Comparing the attitudes of newly hired analysts before unit training with those of long-serving analysts and transferees would show whether techniques instill values or filter people who already hold them.

## Reading R11

**The reading.** What the paper calls occupational values are, at this site, partly the residue of sorting inside one lab. Analysts with the same degrees are placed where there is an opening, can be reassigned by the director, and can lobby to be retrained; several of those now in narcotics had left toxicology because they could not stand its routine. Their recoil from instrument-first drug testing, and the toxicologists' ease with error figures, would then describe who ended up at which bench as much as what each kind of work teaches. A forced change of method would be met with transfer requests as well as resistance, a response the paper's embrace-or-resist pair leaves out. Analysts arrive with the same kinds of degree and are hired into a particular unit after tests and a panel interview (p. 613). Placement is the employer's decision about similar recruits. All are [6 words of Bechky (2020), p. 619] are treated as interchangeable; the director plans to swap two analysts between narcotics and firearms and tells staff that each of them ought to be deployable anywhere in the lab (pp. 619–620). The units appear as postings. An analyst frames her liking for crystal tests as personal: other people would prefer the instrument and she is not one of them (p. 632). Preference is attached to persons. Interviews covered career histories (p. 614). The material that would test the sorting account was gathered. The paper leaves open how neighboring occupations should be bounded (p. 636). **Its opening.** If unit cultures reflect who was placed in or transferred between benches inside one laboratory, then the paper's contrast across neighboring occupations may capture internal staffing sorting across job postings rather than distinct occupational traditions. Examining the full set of career-history interviews and transfer records across the laboratory would show how much unit value differences stem from selective mobility and whether forced method changes trigger transfer requests.

## Reading R13

**The reading.** Read this way, values did not sort the DNA comparison into welcome or threat in advance; they were the language in which a response settled by exposure and feasibility was narrated afterward. Toxicologists changed because a court challenge loomed and a plan existed, then presented accuracy as what they had always prized. Narcotics analysts could not shift to instrumentation with two machines and a statutory turnaround clock; the praise of craft followed. Firearms examiners unchallenged at home called holism a virtue, while those who had faced admissibility hearings elsewhere urged examining bias. **Its opening.** If courtroom exposure and material feasibility settled what each unit could do before analysts narrated those actions in the language of occupational values, then values are a consequence of each unit's response rather than its filter. Tracking a single unit's value talk before and after it encounters a local courtroom challenge or receives new instrument capacity would show whether value claims precede or follow changes in practice.

## Reading R15

**The reading.** The toxicology episode could be read as the accelerated completion of an existing accreditation project. The laboratory already possessed the data, procedures, and plan needed to report measurement uncertainty; an impending courtroom challenge supplied a deadline. DNA supplied a persuasive comparison, but might not have determined the reform's content or eventual adoption. On this reading, the central change was when an established obligation became urgent, rather than which neighboring technology became exemplary.
