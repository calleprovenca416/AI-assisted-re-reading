# The claims of Bechky, B. A. 2020. Evaluative spillovers from technological change: The effects of "DNA envy" on occupational practices in forensic science. Administrative Science Quarterly, 65(3): 606–643.

The paper's 23 claims as its index records them, numbered, each with the pages where the paper
makes it and its statement. A statement is a summary, not the paper's words: check the page.

**C01** (p. 606; pp. 607–608; p. 634; p. 638). Evaluative spillovers allow a technology to reshape the work of adjacent occupations even when those groups do not adopt the technology.

**C02** (p. 606; pp. 607–608; pp. 615–617; p. 619; p. 634). When one forensic technique acquires unusually strong legitimacy, it becomes a benchmark that draws evaluation toward neighboring forensic occupations.

**C03** (p. 606; p. 608; p. 617; p. 620; pp. 624–625; pp. 627–628; p. 632; pp. 633–636). Institutional pathways shape how evaluative spillovers are encountered and acted on; locally immediate routes create a stronger impetus for local practice change than remote or nonurgent routes.

**C04** (p. 606; p. 608; p. 617; p. 620; p. 625; pp. 627–628; p. 632; pp. 633–637). The technical compatibility between a benchmark technology and an occupation’s existing method shapes how feasible adaptation is, although compatibility by itself does not guarantee change.

**C05** (p. 606; p. 608; p. 617; p. 620; p. 625; pp. 627–628; p. 632; pp. 633–637). Occupational values filter evaluative comparisons: alignment with valued ways of doing good work supports adaptation, whereas a threat to those values promotes resistance.

**C06** (pp. 608–609). Direct adoption of new technology can reconfigure occupational task boundaries, access to resources, workplace relations, and status arrangements.

**C07** (pp. 608–609). The same technology can yield different workplace social arrangements because local expertise, routines, and relationships condition how it is used.

**C08** (pp. 608–609). Occupations with greater professional authority are better positioned to resist technology-driven changes to their tasks.

**C09** (p. 610). Association with novel technology can raise an occupation’s symbolic legitimacy because technology is culturally linked to progress and rationality.

**C10** (p. 611; p. 615; p. 620; p. 637). Differences in occupational work and field relationships generate distinct occupational cultures, including different values and preferred practices.

**C11** (p. 611). Changes in occupational practice are mediated by how workers make sense of institutional demands.

**C12** (p. 613). Ethnographic observation can make visible how organization members interpret and respond to field-level discourse in ways that other methods may not reveal.

**C13** (p. 617; p. 634). A benchmark technology is not interpreted as one fixed standard; neighboring occupations select different features of it when comparing the technology with their own work.

**C14** (pp. 618–619). Statistical conventions, error reporting, reviewable outputs, and iterative review in DNA work transform a messy judgment-heavy process into evidence that outsiders can perceive as objective and credible.

**C15** (p. 619). The privileged legitimacy and resource position of DNA profiling generated “DNA envy” and resentment elsewhere in forensic science.

**C16** (pp. 624–625; pp. 633–637). At MCCL, firearms examiners resisted local practice change because the comparison arrived through remote challenges, did not map onto their method, and conflicted with their commitment to holistic subjective judgment.

**C17** (p. 625; pp. 627–628; pp. 633–637). At MCCL, toxicologists accelerated a reporting change because a local court threat was immediate, the change fit their instrumented method and error-avoidance values, and related accreditation work was already underway.

**C18** (p. 628; p. 632; pp. 633–637). At MCCL, narcotics analysts retained crystal-testing practices because the pressure came through an internal professional debate without immediate compulsion and the implied move to instrumentation threatened autonomy, variety, and craft judgment despite technical compatibility.

**C19** (p. 634; p. 636). Evaluative spillovers are more likely to reach occupations that share evaluating audiences and have overlapping activities or interests; weaker overlap should reduce spillover.

**C20** (p. 620; p. 637). Occupational values and technique are intertwined in practice because values are rooted in what the work requires and reveals to practitioners.

**C21** (p. 637). For the three focal occupations, differences in the nature of the work—especially values and technique—accounted for the response pattern more than relative occupational status or power did.

**C22** (p. 638). Applying the paper’s framework beyond forensics, ethnographers are expected to resist pressure to alter masking practices because source anonymity is strongly valued and the comparison reaches them through a relatively weak public route.

**C23** (p. 638). As digital technologies affect more workplaces, evaluative spillovers are expected to travel across a wider range of occupations and industries.
