# The claims of Pratt, M. G. 2000. The good, the bad, and the ambivalent: Managing identification among Amway distributors. Administrative Science Quarterly, 45(3): 456–493.

The paper's 60 claims as its index records them, numbered, each with the pages where the paper
makes it and its statement. A statement is a summary, not the paper's words: check the page.

**C01** (pp. 456–457, 462–463, 481; Figure 2, p. 482). Amway manages how members define their connection to the organization through the paired processes labeled sensebreaking and sensegiving.

**C02** (p. 456; pp. 476–477; p. 483). Positive identification is fostered when sensebreaking and sensegiving both work successfully.

**C03** (p. 456; pp. 477–481; p. 483). Breakdowns in sensebreaking or sensegiving can shift members away from positive identification toward broken, oppositional, or mixed forms of attachment.

**C04** (p. 456). As organizations rely less on direct supervision and conventional workplace cues, they have greater reason to use shared values as a means of coordinating members.

**C05** (p. 457). Organizational identification is distinguished from commitment and person-organization fit by making the self-concept central to the member-organization connection.

**C06** (p. 462; p. 485). The emotional valence of a person’s reaction to Amway differentiates identification states: positive, negative, mixed, and weak reactions align with positive, oppositional, ambivalent, and absent forms of identification.

**C07** (pp. 462–463; Figure 1, p. 477). Among distributors, differences in identification track whether dream building and positive programming succeed or fail.

**C08** (p. 463). Prospecting did not distinguish members’ identification because everyone studied as a member had already been recruited successfully.

**C09** (p. 463). Financial performance did not determine whether distributors identified positively, negatively, ambivalently, or weakly with Amway.

**C10** (p. 463). Visible demographic differences did not appear to sort distributors into distinct identification states.

**C11** (pp. 463–464, 481, 484). The paper explains identification states as products of motivated sensemaking about the self and its organizational connection.

**C12** (pp. 464, 469). Successful sensebreaking creates seekership by unsettling a member’s current understanding of self and producing a need for new meaning.

**C13** (pp. 464–469). Dream building serves as Amway’s principal sensebreaking practice by tying aspirations to the self, creating identity deficits, and keeping the resulting motivation active.

**C14** (p. 466). Personalizing dreams around familiar choices and emotionally attractive possibilities makes those aspirations easier for distributors to fold into their sense of self.

**C15** (pp. 467–468). Comparing a desired future self with the current self creates an identity deficit that motivates distributors to alter the present self.

**C16** (p. 467). Identity deficits exert stronger motivational pressure when people are already dissatisfied with their present identity or are in crisis.

**C17** (pp. 468–469). Repeated dream building keeps members in seekership by replacing fulfilled aspirations with new ones and renewing the need for self-validation.

**C18** (pp. 468–469). As dream building continues, additional areas of a distributor’s life become tied to the distributor identity.

**C19** (p. 469). Seekership directs members to look for interpretations that can resolve uncertainty about who they are.

**C20** (pp. 469–476). Positive programming channels seekers toward supportive Amway relationships and away from disfavored outside influences; when it works, this produces encapsulated sensemaking.

**C21** (pp. 470–471). Upline-downline bonds become stronger when mentors enact both practical help with the business and personal care for the distributor.

**C22** (pp. 472–474). Revaluing outside relationships against the Amway relationship standard creates barriers that increase social and ideological encapsulation.

**C23** (pp. 473–475; p. 487). Encapsulation shifts sensemaking toward supportive organizational sources and reduces the force of negative outside evaluations.

**C24** (p. 474). Freely chosen acts of distributing can strengthen commitment to those acts and make the distributor identity more prominent in later interpretation.

**C25** (p. 474). Recruiting interactions can become self-reinforcing because either acceptance or rejection by friends and family can be interpreted as support for the Amway relationship standard.

**C26** (p. 476; p. 483). Once positive identification forms, it encourages continued dream building and repeated enactment of the identification process.

**C27** (pp. 477–478; p. 483). When sensebreaking fails to create or sustain seekership, identification does not form or an existing identification dissolves.

**C28** (pp. 478–479; p. 483). When sensebreaking succeeds but sensegiving fails, members are pushed toward disidentification, in part because they turn to Amway-critical outsiders for meaning.

**C29** (pp. 479–481; p. 483). When sensebreaking succeeds but sensegiving is only partly effective, members draw meaning from both Amway supporters and critics and develop ambivalent identification.

**C30** (p. 482). Selecting people already inclined to seek meaning through organizations may make organizational identification easier to manage.

**C31** (p. 483). Organizations that attract favorable reactions from outsiders may need less rigid insider-outsider boundaries because supportive nonmembers can also supply organization-consistent meaning.

**C32** (p. 483). Positive identification is expected to make members more willing to follow what the organization asks of them.

**C33** (p. 483). Positive identification should also make members more receptive to later rounds of sensebreaking and sensegiving.

**C34** (p. 483; p. 485). Identification management is an ongoing process because identification can be reenacted, weakened, or transformed rather than fixed once and for all.

**C35** (p. 484; p. 487). A discrepancy between the current and ideal self creates a need for meaning that can motivate organizational identification.

**C36** (p. 484). Identification can become a frame through which later experience is interpreted, even though identification itself emerges from sensemaking.

**C37** (p. 484). Organizational identification depends not only on a person’s relation to an abstract organization but also on how the self is interpreted through relationships with members and nonmembers.

**C38** (p. 485). As more domains of a person’s identity become bound to the organization, the identification becomes deeper.

**C39** (pp. 486–487). Professional socialization may build strong professional identification when practices that unsettle the self are paired with relationships and demands that channel subsequent meaning-making.

**C40** (p. 487). For geographically dispersed workforces, encapsulated sensemaking may help preserve a shared purpose despite limited face-to-face contact.

**C41** (p. 487). Strong positive identification can improve organizational functioning.

**C42** (pp. 487–488). Intensive identification management can also impose costs on members and organizations, including dependence, conformity, burnout, reduced flexibility, and turnover.

**C43** (p. 488). For many active distributors, Amway gave their lives greater purpose and communal belonging by bringing work, family, and religion into one identity framework.

**C44** (p. 456). Strong organizational cultures may help sustain members’ loyalty and commitment when job security no longer provides that attachment.

**C45** (p. 458). Extreme-case research can aid theory development because the focal dynamics are easier to see in unusually strong settings.

**C46** (p. 460). Using several kinds of data enables triangulation that can strengthen the paper’s interpretive assertions.

**C47** (p. 460). Participating in the same work as informants can increase the trust they place in the researcher.

**C48** (p. 460). A semiovert researcher role can create room to ask questions that would be harder to pose as an ordinary coworker.

**C49** (footnote 14, p. 474). Relationship configurations created by positive programming can remain balanced whether prospects join or refuse, and that stability may reinforce organization-consistent sensemaking.

**C50** (p. 487). In part-time, contingent, or voluntary membership settings, creating seekership may give people a stronger reason to form an organizational attachment.

**C51** (p. 487). In professions with lengthy socialization, seekership may help sustain motivation for continued development after formal qualification.

**C52** (pp. 487–488). Identification-management practices that can generate substantial turnover are more workable for organizations with a large supply of potential labor.

**C53** (footnote 6, p. 460). Learning an organization’s culture is especially concentrated in the initial six months after entry.

**C54** (footnote 11, p. 467). Goals tied to a person’s view of who they are can exert an unusually strong influence on their willingness to act.

**C55** (footnote 13, p. 473). Amway’s classifications of insiders and outsiders can shift with recruitment interest, members’ reduced activity, and the position of unsupportive spouses.

**C56** (p. 477). A system that does not consistently secure identification among members leaves the organization more exposed to member departure.

**C57** (p. 478). When members have no ongoing need to pursue personal change through Amway, they are less inclined to consult or bond with upline distributors.

**C58** (p. 479). Combining business interests with personal closeness can make mentoring ties especially vulnerable to conflict or resentment.

**C59** (pp. 481–482; Figure 2, p. 482). Organizational practices that undermine a member’s current self-evaluation can create seekership without relying on Amway-style dream building.

**C60** (Figure 2, p. 482; pp. 482–483). Organizations may also foster encapsulated sensemaking by shaping group cohesion, insider distinctions, social contacts, and favorable outsider relationships.
