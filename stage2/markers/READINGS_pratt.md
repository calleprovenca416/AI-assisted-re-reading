# Readings of Pratt, M. G. 2000. The good, the bad, and the ambivalent: Managing identification among Amway distributors. Administrative Science Quarterly, 45(3): 456–493.

15 readings of this paper, in no particular order. Each is split
into numbered sentences. A page in a sentence is a pointer, not a proof.

## Reading P01

P01-01  The outcome is decided outside Amway, by how a member's existing circle responds when approached.

P01-02  Where family and friends join or at least tolerate the venture, the member's world fills with supporters and looks encapsulated.

P01-03  Where valued others recoil and the member is unwilling to lose them, the member hedges, hides and stalls.

P01-04  Where the member's confidants are hostile, the member turns against the organisation.

P01-05  Encapsulation is then something outsiders do to distributors as much as something positive programming does to members.

P01-06  Non-members told the author they avoid distributor friends and relatives, and a distributor calls the first months the hardest because of how intimates react (p. 474).

P01-07  The barrier is built from both sides.

P01-08  Bringing family and friends in is said to secure [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 473] (p. 473).

P01-09  Who says yes determines the member's surroundings.

P01-10  A friend schedules his wedding against an Amway event, and unsupportive spouses are a named category needing continued persuasion (p. 472).

P01-11  Outsiders are active parties.

P01-12  One ambivalent distributor [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 480] (p. 480).

P01-13  Another disowns Amway on the spot when a co-worker repeats a slur about it.

P01-14  The stalling follows an outsider's reaction.

P01-15  Ambivalent members restrict their calls to acquaintances outside daily life and agonise about exploiting close ties (p. 481).

P01-16  Footnote 16 has the author's own enthusiasm sagging around critical colleagues.

P01-17  Disidentified former members made sense of their experience with confidants who were firmly negative (p. 479).

P01-18  The valence of the outcome matches the valence of the audience.

## Reading P02

P02-01  Start the episode at the recruitment meeting and the second disidentified informant's story is complete before the paper's episode opens.

P02-02  He describes how his sponsor got him in: a sales talk, people around him saying positive things, a slide show, being there in someone else's car, strong social pressure, and paying about $120 as the easiest way out; he says he felt humiliated and likens it to a torture session (p. 479).

P02-03  In that passage the unfavourable reading of the sponsor is already in place at the moment of joining, and joining is described as an exit from the room, not as the pursuit of anything wanted.

P02-04  Nothing printed for him shows a dream, an upline relationship that then went wrong, or a confidant.

P02-05  The recruitment meeting is also not empty of the practices the paper studies: an Emerald distributor addresses a group of prospects at a meeting that is also a dream-building session (p. 466), and the first disidentified informant locates what attracted him at the outset, in the look of his recruiter's home (p. 478).

P02-06  On this reading recruitment is where an identification in opposition can be set, and 'successfully recruited' covers both a prospect who was drawn in (p. 478) and one who paid to get out (p. 479).

## Reading P03

P03-01  Seekership is brought to Amway, not made by it.

P03-02  Members who identify, for or against, arrive already discontented with their job, money or family situation.

P03-03  Dream building gives that discontent a vocabulary rather than breaking a settled sense of self.

P03-04  Those who drift away are those whose outside lives were, or became, satisfactory.

P03-05  On this reading the first branch of the model sorts people by prior circumstance, not by whether a practice succeeded.

P03-06  The paper says identity deficits bite hardest on people in crisis or unhappy with who they are (p. 467).

P03-07  This concedes that prior state conditions the effect.

P03-08  The success-story distributor credits her upline with carrying her through a divorce (p. 470).

P03-09  The trouble predates the bond.

P03-10  Testimonies of an upline helping one member off drugs and feeding a couple in need (p. 471).

P03-11  Again need precedes attachment.

P03-12  In Table 1 the family dream is voiced by a man whose wife is worn out by her job and whose child is in day care all week (p. 465).

P03-13  The dream reads as a description of an existing strain.

P03-14  An ambivalent distributor frames his dream as escaping a job where others control whether he works (p. 480).

P03-15  This is a grievance about present employment.

P03-16  The non-renewer explains leaving by saying her job is now going well and she likes it (pp. 477–478).

P03-17  Deidentification here follows outside contentment.

P03-18  The general model adds a selection box and allows that some people are predisposed to seek, through dissatisfaction or disposition (p. 482).

P03-19  The paper thus names this route itself but does not test it on its own informants.

P03-20  Footnote 9 admits individual differences cannot be ruled out (p. 463).

P03-21  The design did not collect what would rule them out.

## Reading P04

P04-01  The pair is the analyst's, a weaker footing than one the paper drew.

P04-02  As printed, each passage has a before and an after, and in each the before runs against the state the episode is filed under.

P04-03  The distributor in the passage on p. 476 had ignored her upline's advice in that recruiting attempt; the conclusion that the upline and the recordings had foreseen her friends' reaction came after.

P04-04  The distributor in the passage on p. 480 had Amway merchandise on display at work; the denial came after a coworker tied Amway to Nazis.

P04-05  The paper's account gives the first a reliance on upline sources (p. 475, the author's account) and the second a standing embarrassment (the author's characterisation on p. 480).

P04-06  The passages print people being moved by an event, the direction the paper's claim (p. 484) leaves open: the episode first, the stance after.

P04-07  The two responses are also not the same kind of thing.

P04-08  The passage on p. 480 prints what a distributor did at once, on the remark.

P04-09  The passage on p. 476 prints what a distributor concluded afterwards and told the researcher.

P04-10  Two other passages filed with the passage on p. 476 are of the second kind: a confrontation retold in celebration at a dinner (p. 475), and friends' avoidance retold as a lesson about those friends (p. 475).

P04-11  In the two places where the paper prints the moment of facing an outsider's doubt about Amway, it prints giving way: the denial in the passage on p. 480, and the researcher's enthusiasm weakening among skeptical colleagues, whom he then avoided (p. 481).

P04-12  The wager is that the pair shows two moments of one sequence and not two kinds of member.

P04-13  In front of the outsider the member gives ground; afterwards, away from the outsider, the organization's account turns the same blow into a point in its favor.

P04-14  What the paper files as a mixed state would be the first moment, and what it files as reliance on upline sources the second.

P04-15  The second moment is close to what the paper itself claims (pp. 473–475, p. 487); the difference is that the paper sorts members and this reading sorts moments.

## Reading P05

P05-01  There is one practice, not two.

P05-02  The upline relationship does all the work, and dream building is something done inside it.

P05-03  Dreams are supplied, shaped and kept alive by the mentor, so a 'successful' dream is evidence of a working bond and a faded dream is evidence of a bond that never formed or went sour.

P05-04  The paper's two-by-two logic then collapses into a single dimension, the quality of the tie to the upline, with ambivalence as its middle value.

P05-05  Members learn what to dream mainly by modelling [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 465] people in their own line (p. 465).

P05-06  The content of the dream comes from the upline.

P05-07  Upline and downline jointly craft the individual dream from a finite approved menu (p. 466).

P05-08  Dream building is described as a relational activity.

P05-09  In the appendix the Emerald chooses the shops, the colours and the items, and the author likes almost nothing all day (pp. 492–493).

P05-10  Meanwhile a joking father-son exchange occurs.

P05-11  The session produces a bond more visibly than a want.

P05-12  A distributor wants to reach Pearl in order to golf with his upline because he likes them (p. 471).

P05-13  The dream's object is the relationship.

P05-14  An interviewee says those who stay active are those who keep [7 words of Pratt (2000), p. 475] (p. 475).

## Reading P06

P06-01  The concept is doing two jobs.

P06-02  X is a stated current dissatisfaction with one's own life; Y is excitement about a dream with no such dissatisfaction stated.

P06-03  The printed passages let them come apart.

P06-04  The author's own journal reports being caught up in a dream of personal freedom while also thinking the dreams foolish, and names nothing wrong with his present life (p. 466).

P06-05  A former distributor says they were excited at the beginning about their own business and financial independence and then lost interest (p. 477): Y occurred and lapsed, and X is not reported.

P06-06  The distributor who did not renew gives her reason as a present life she likes, a job that has reached the point of little overtime (p. 477): X is absent, and nothing is said about dreams.

P06-07  Where X is printed, the speaker ties it to paid work or to things lacked: a wife burned out at her job, two working parents and [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 465] (p. 465); not wanting to be at the mercy of people at work (p. 480); not having what the recruiter had (p. 478).

P06-08  The wager is that X, not Y, is what goes with an identification of any kind forming, and that the printed passages show Y as what sessions, tapes and displays deliver while showing X in speakers' accounts of their jobs and households.

P06-09  If so, 'sensebreaking failed' on p. 477 covers two different things: excitement that was produced and faded, and a present life that was never, or no longer, found wanting.

## Reading P07

P07-01  Read the other way round, not distributing comes first and the intact non-member ties are its result.

P07-02  No printed passage from an ambivalent distributor reports being advised against Amway by a non-member.

P07-03  The one informant who addresses the point says nobody tells him he cannot or should not do it, that everybody says it works, and that the not-starting is down to him (p. 480).

P07-04  What the other passages print is abstention: one inactive distributor does not bring lists, does not sponsor and does not use the products consistently (p. 480); another now calls only people he does not see much of (p. 480).

P07-05  On the author's own account, approaching family and friends is how a distributor finds out which relationships are negative (author's characterisation on p. 474; an active distributor describes the first months of approaching them as the hardest, p. 474).

P07-06  A distributor who does not approach never runs that test, so nothing has happened that would lead to those ties being dropped.

P07-07  On this reading the ambivalence printed on p. 480 is between wanting to start and not starting, not between two sets of advisers, and the kept ties are a trace of the inactivity rather than its cause.

## Reading P08

P08-02  The paper prints that no current or former distributor interviewed gave a clear answer on net profit or loss (p. 463), and that informants disagreed on whether tapes, books and functions count as costs (p. 463).

P08-03  The one financial marker it names for the people it calls successful is [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 463] (p. 463).

P08-04  What the two observations on p. 463 establish, then, is that identification type did not follow sales volume.

P08-05  Each informant's net position, and which way money moved between that informant and the people above them, is not observed for anyone.

P08-06  That unobserved quantity is what the printed disidentification material is about.

P08-07  One disidentified informant describes paying about $120 at his recruitment as the way out of a pressured sale (p. 479).

P08-08  The author's own summary of how disidentified distributors re-read upline warmth is that upline were being pleasant in order to make money for themselves (author's characterisation on p. 479).

P08-09  Members pay a fee of about $[7 words of Pratt (2000), p. 459] (p. 459).

P08-10  An upline tells a salesperson in the author's hearing that the author will be rich soon or the upline is wasting his time (p. 493), and the author reports that those above him cared only that he was selling and trying to sponsor (p. 460 n. 5).

P08-11  The wager is that the ending could go the other way on a narrower question: whether a member comes out as someone money flows from or someone money flows to may sort with the direction of their identification, and the comparison on p. 463 could not have detected it.

## Reading P09

P09-01  The paper's hedge makes leaving the end that the practices come to when they fail on a member.

P09-02  Read as provisional, the departures in print are not that end; they come before the practices have run.

P09-03  Every former distributor interviewed had [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 461] (p. 461).

P09-04  Their accounts are of an early appeal that faded (p. 477), of something tried that did not suit (p. 478), of a regular job that became satisfying (pp. 477–478), of buying in to get out of an embarrassing situation at a presentation (p. 479).

P09-05  The printed figures agree that staying active is not the usual course: a secondary source puts yearly leaving between about two-fifths and nearly all of the membership (p. 477), and Amway's own publication counts fewer than half of recorded distributors as active (p. 463).

P09-06  The practices, meanwhile, are printed as working by repetition and over time: an achieved dream is to be replaced by a larger one (p. 468), no achievement is to be taken as a final peak (p. 468), successful distributors' aspirations shifted over time (p. 468), and by the author's account encapsulation grew as members spent more time with other distributors (p. 473).

P09-07  On this reading the order in the paper's claim (p. 477) turns over.

P09-08  The leavers did not go because identification failed to take; they went early, before the repeated work the paper describes had been done on them, and identification was not secured because they had gone.

P09-09  Leaving in the first year is then one thing and the failure of the practices another, and the leavers in print speak only to the first.

P09-10  Where the practices fail would show in people who left after long, active membership.

P09-11  The paper comes near such people twice and prints no account from either: distributors who reached high sales levels before quitting (p. 463), whose length of membership is not given, and distributors who moved away from positive identification (p. 485), of whom it is not said whether any left.

## Reading P10

P10-01  The pair says the opposed leaver kept the wish for change and the detached one lost it.

P10-02  The passages on the opposed side do not line up that way.

P10-03  One former distributor was drawn by a successful member's large home because he lacked such possessions (p. 478) and describes becoming a lawyer as a dream worth hard work (p. 478): the wish is printed for him, and nothing about his upline is.

P10-04  The former distributor in the passage on p. 479 blames his upline for not recognizing the sales ability he came with and for relying on more meetings instead of finding a way to motivate him: no wish he was pursuing is printed, only a man who by his own telling still needed motivating.

P10-05  A third, whom the author files under the same opposed outcome, says he bought in only to get out of an embarrassing situation at a high-pressure presentation (p. 479); no passage gives him a dream.

P10-06  No passage says that the man of the passages on p. 478 is the man of the passage on p. 479.

P10-07  The upline tie does not separate the two sides either.

P10-08  By the author's account the detached formed no strong bonds with distributors (p. 478), and those who came to disidentify are reported with sparse and distant upline relationships (p. 479).

P10-09  The paper puts that weak tie after the loss of desire on the one side (p. 478) and before the opposition on the other (p. 479, the author's account); this reading puts it after on both.

P10-10  What the passages on p. 479 print, and the passage on pp. 477–478 does not, is a wrong: something done to the speaker by the people who brought him in or stood above him.

P10-11  The passage on pp. 477–478 has no Amway person in it; what changed was her regular job.

P10-12  The wager is that a leaver ends opposed when he carries such a wrong out with him, whether or not the wish for change survived, and ends detached when he carries none.

## Reading P11

P11-01  This group speaks in one passage, so the retelling is short.

P11-02  Asked for their net profit, current and former distributors did two things: they did not produce a clear figure, and they disagreed among themselves about which product margins and which training expenses belong in the sum (p. 463).

P11-03  The paper takes this as a limit on what could be measured and goes on to conclude that money did not sort people (the paper's claim at p. 463).

P11-04  Told from the distributors' side it is a report about them.

P11-05  Among the people doing this work, what the business earns is not a settled number; it depends on what one is prepared to call a cost.

P11-06  The cost in dispute is not a small one.

P11-07  Distributors paid for their training (p. 459), and the training is printed as a system of books, recordings, meetings, rallies and seminars (p. 459); books and tapes are also among the media through which members met model dreams (pp. 465–466).

P11-08  Whoever leaves those expenses out has a business that nets more than whoever puts them in.

P11-09  The wager is that the disagreement in the passage on p. 463 ran between those who stood with [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 462], and that counting the training as a cost of the business, or not counting it, is itself a way of standing toward the organization.

P11-10  If that is so, financial result cannot be laid beside identification as a separate measure, as the passage on p. 463 lays it, since a distributor's own figure already holds an answer to what the training is.

P11-11  This does not dispute that money failed to sort people; it disputes that money was a separate thing to sort them by.

P11-12  The passage does not say along what line the disagreement ran, and it leaves 'could not' and 'would not' unseparated.

## Reading P12

P12-02  What it shows is not that finances are irrelevant but that members cannot or will not reckon them, and that the unreckoned items are precisely what flows up the line: tapes, books, functions, fees.

P12-03  Read this way, positive identification is the state of not having done the sum.

P12-04  Disidentification is the state of having done it and concluded that the warmth was commercial.

P12-05  Inactivity is the cheap holding position of paying only the renewal.

P12-06  The identification types are stances toward a ledger.

P12-07  No current or former distributor could, or would, state net profit, and they disagreed on whether training materials count as costs (p. 463).

P12-08  The paper reports this as a surprise and moves on.

P12-09  Here it is the central finding.

P12-10  The company's own figure for average monthly gross income of an active distributor is very low, and footnote 8 says nearly all actives fall short of high volume (p. 463).

P12-11  Most members are spending, not earning.

P12-12  Members pay a joining fee and often pay for books and tapes (p. 459).

P12-13  The costs are structural.

P12-14  Mentors direct which books to read and tapes to buy (p. 471).

P12-15  The sensegiver is also the person steering purchases.

P12-16  Disidentified members reinterpret upline kindness as a means to the upline's own income and feel used (p. 479).

P12-17  Their turn is an economic re-reading of the relationship.

P12-18  One former member recalls paying the entry sum as [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 479] pressured pitch (p. 479).

P12-19  His grievance begins with a payment.

P12-20  Inactive distributors are defined as those who renew annually but do little else (p. 461).

P13-13  The tie in the passage on p. 479 is the ordinary, work-bound one, meeting a man it did not move.

## Reading P15

P15-01  Followed through Mark, the session prints three things happening, and a wanted possession for the downline is the slightest of them.

P15-02  First, Mark pursues his own wants: he points out a leather jacket, says he is interested in it himself, and arranges for the clerk to phone the author so that the author can buy it for him when it is reduced (p. 492); he asks about boot shops and goes to one although he knows the author has no interest in boots, and says his wife gets angry when he does this on such outings (p. 493).

P15-03  Second, Mark shows and coaches a manner: he takes control in the first shop, stumps a jeweller with knowledgeable questions (p. 492), tells the two downline that their ignorance of jewellery shows and that they should fake it, praises the author for walking straight in at the next shop, and says [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 493] get treated well (p. 493).

P15-04  Third, Mark works the relationship and the business: he offers to pick up cheaper shorts near his home (p. 492), buys the author a biscuit with a father-and-son joke that the author returns, tells a salesperson the author will soon be rich or else Mark is wasting his time, and begins prospecting that salesperson (p. 493).

P15-05  Against this, the author records declining interest in men's jewellery (p. 492), little success in several clothing shops, being worn out (p. 493), and liking very few things all day, the jacket Mark had pointed out being one of them (p. 492).

P15-06  Mark's own stated view is that most people do not know what they want and learn it from watching the upline want things (p. 493).

P15-07  So in this exhibit what passes to the downline is mainly how Mark carries himself and a familial tie to Mark; the mentor relationship the paper places second is being built inside the practice it places first, and the standard set before the downline is the mentor more than any object.

P15-08  This sits close to what the author concedes on pp. 481–482, that the two kinds of practice blur as other distributors come to shape one's ideal self; it differs in holding that, in the one session printed, the blur is there from the first outing rather than arriving later.
