# Openings of the readings of Pratt, M. G. 2000. The good, the bad, and the ambivalent: Managing identification among Amway distributors. Administrative Science Quarterly, 45(3): 456–493.

The readings of this paper, in their order, each followed by its opening: two sentences another reader wrote, the first on what the reading opens in the paper's argument, the second on the next investigation that would settle it.

## Reading R01

**The reading.** The four identification types are stations on a membership career, not separate products of practices succeeding or failing. Entry brings a burst of enthusiasm. The first year brings a crisis in which many leave, indifferent or angry. Survivors either deepen or settle into a long, low-cost stall. The branching diagram is then a cross-section of people caught at different points on one time course, and 'success' and 'failure' of practices name where someone currently stands. All former distributors interviewed had left within a year, inactive ones had been members for at least a year and a half, and actives ranged up to two decades (p. 461). Exit clusters early and inactivity appears only after the first year. A distributor singles out the first few months as the hardest (p. 474). There is a recognised early trial. A former distributor describes initial excitement followed by fading interest (p. 477). This is a sequence within one person, which the paper files as a type. Among the successful, dreams shift over time from lifestyle to family to saving the country (p. 468). Positive identification itself has stages. Footnote 13 notes that members who stop selling are kept in the fold for a long time before being dropped (p. 473). The organisation treats inactivity as a phase. Ambivalent members cycle between bursts of activity and long pauses (p. 481). Their state is described as movement. The paper states that distributors moved between identification states in several directions but does not discuss this in detail (p. 485). The trajectories exist in the data and are not printed.

**Its opening.** If exit clusters in the first year while inactivity appears only after eighteen months, the branching model may be a cross-section of members at different tenure stages rather than a map of socialization practices succeeding or failing. Reconstructing the full longitudinal state-transition histories mentioned on page 485 against member tenure would test whether distributors pass through enthusiasm, crisis, stall, or deepening commitment in a regular temporal sequence.

## Reading R02

**The reading.** The pair is the analyst's, a weaker footing than one the paper drew. As printed, each passage has a before and an after, and in each the before runs against the state the episode is filed under. The distributor in the passage on p. 476 had ignored her upline's advice in that recruiting attempt; the conclusion that the upline and the recordings had foreseen her friends' reaction came after. The distributor in the passage on p. 480 had Amway merchandise on display at work; the denial came after a coworker tied Amway to Nazis. The paper's account gives the first a reliance on upline sources (p. 475, the author's account) and the second a standing embarrassment (the author's characterisation on p. 480). The passages print people being moved by an event, the direction the paper's claim (p. 484) leaves open: the episode first, the stance after. The two responses are also not the same kind of thing. The passage on p. 480 prints what a distributor did at once, on the remark. The passage on p. 476 prints what a distributor concluded afterwards and told the researcher. Two other passages filed with the passage on p. 476 are of the second kind: a confrontation retold in celebration at a dinner (p. 475), and friends' avoidance retold as a lesson about those friends (p. 475). In the two places where the paper prints the moment of facing an outsider's doubt about Amway, it prints giving way: the denial in the passage on p. 480, and the researcher's enthusiasm weakening among skeptical colleagues, whom he then avoided (p. 481). The wager is that the pair shows two moments of one sequence and not two kinds of member. In front of the outsider the member gives ground; afterwards, away from the outsider, the organization's account turns the same blow into a point in its favor. What the paper files as a mixed state would be the first moment, and what it files as reliance on upline sources the second. The second moment is close to what the paper itself claims (pp. 473–475, p. 487); the difference is that the paper sorts members and this reading sorts moments.

**Its opening.** Because the evidence for ambivalent yielding records what distributors did on the spot in front of critics while the evidence for encapsulation records how distributors retold encounters afterward, the contrast may capture two consecutive moments of one interaction rather than two types of members. Observing strongly identified distributors in real time when outsiders disparage Amway, and tracking how distributors who disown Amway at work later recount the incident to their sponsors, would show whether encapsulation prevents public yielding or repairs it afterward.

## Reading R03

**The reading.** The paper's hedge makes leaving the end that the practices come to when they fail on a member. Read as provisional, the departures in print are not that end; they come before the practices have run. Every former distributor interviewed had [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 461] (p. 461). Their accounts are of an early appeal that faded (p. 477), of something tried that did not suit (p. 478), of a regular job that became satisfying (pp. 477–478), of buying in to get out of an embarrassing situation at a presentation (p. 479). The printed figures agree that staying active is not the usual course: a secondary source puts yearly leaving between about two-fifths and nearly all of the membership (p. 477), and Amway's own publication counts fewer than half of recorded distributors as active (p. 463). The practices, meanwhile, are printed as working by repetition and over time: an achieved dream is to be replaced by a larger one (p. 468), no achievement is to be taken as a final peak (p. 468), successful distributors' aspirations shifted over time (p. 468), and by the author's account encapsulation grew as members spent more time with other distributors (p. 473). On this reading the order in the paper's claim (p. 477) turns over. The leavers did not go because identification failed to take; they went early, before the repeated work the paper describes had been done on them, and identification was not secured because they had gone. Leaving in the first year is then one thing and the failure of the practices another, and the leavers in print speak only to the first. Where the practices fail would show in people who left after long, active membership. The paper comes near such people twice and prints no account from either: distributors who reached high sales levels before quitting (p. 463), whose length of membership is not given, and distributors who moved away from positive identification (p. 485), of whom it is not said whether any left.

**Its opening.** Because dream building and social encapsulation work through cumulative repetition over time while every interviewed former member quit within twelve months, the paper's exit accounts may document early departure prior to socialization rather than the failure of those practices. Interviewing former distributors who left after years of active involvement or after reaching high sales ranks on page 463 would reveal how established identification breaks down once the practices have fully run.

## Reading R04

**The reading.** Start the episode at the recruitment meeting and the second disidentified informant's story is complete before the paper's episode opens. He describes how his sponsor got him in: a sales talk, people around him saying positive things, a slide show, being there in someone else's car, strong social pressure, and paying about $120 as the easiest way out; he says he felt humiliated and likens it to a torture session (p. 479). In that passage the unfavourable reading of the sponsor is already in place at the moment of joining, and joining is described as an exit from the room, not as the pursuit of anything wanted. Nothing printed for him shows a dream, an upline relationship that then went wrong, or a confidant. The recruitment meeting is also not empty of the practices the paper studies: an Emerald distributor addresses a group of prospects at a meeting that is also a dream-building session (p. 466), and the first disidentified informant locates what attracted him at the outset, in the look of his recruiter's home (p. 478). On this reading recruitment is where an identification in opposition can be set, and 'successfully recruited' covers both a prospect who was drawn in (p. 478) and one who paid to get out (p. 479).

**Its opening.** When a prospect pays the entry fee simply to escape intense social pressure in a stranger's car, recruitment itself can generate oppositional disidentification without any prior dream building or subsequent mentoring breakdown. Comparing how informants across all four identification states experienced their initial recruitment pitch would establish whether high-pressure prospecting directly seeds later disidentification.

## Reading R05

**The reading.** Read for what distributors did rather than what they believed, the paper supports a plainer account of positive identification: it is the residue of freely chosen, costly, public action. Dream building and positive programming produce it together because each is an occasion for enactment—trying on suits before one's upline, paying for tapes and functions, approaching relatives first and then withdrawing from them. Freely taken acts bind the actor, and cognition reorganizes to justify them. Identification thus tracks the dose of enactment, is indifferent to financial return, and fails where the committing act was coerced. The meaning void comes afterward, as the committed person's story of why she committed. Removes payoff as the explanation: identification bore no relation to financial success, nobody could state a net profit, and average gross income was trivial — what one expects if attachment grows from money and effort already spent rather than from returns (p. 463). Provides the paper's own aside that as distributors spent more time pursuing dreams, more of their lives became bound up with the distributor identity, which the paper itself labels behavioral commitment; the reading moves this aside to the center of the account (p. 469). States the process in the paper's own terms — enacting positive programming involves choice, freely chosen behavior is highly committing, and behaving as a distributor leads to thinking of oneself as one — and documents the bridge-burning of approaching relatives first and then withdrawing from them, acts hard to reverse that foreclose exits (p. 474). Anchors the outcome and its wording: positive identification appeared when both practices succeeded, is described as continuously enacted and reenacted, and best characterized active distributors; the reading takes the word enacted literally (p. 476). Marks the boundary condition: the former distributor who disidentified recalls being sold under intense social pressure and paying as the easiest way out of an awkward room, then feeling humiliated — an act without volition, which on this reading cannot bind and instead breeds resentment (p. 479).

**Its opening.** If freely chosen, costly actions like buying training materials and alienating skeptical relatives bind distributors behaviorally and force their beliefs to align afterward, then behavioral commitment rather than a prior meaning void produces positive identification. Tracking new recruits over time to see whether expressions of identity discontent precede or follow their first costly public acts of buying materials and approaching relatives would test whether seekership drives enactment or rationalizes it afterward.

## Reading R06

**The reading.** Read the other way round, not distributing comes first and the intact non-member ties are its result. No printed passage from an ambivalent distributor reports being advised against Amway by a non-member. The one informant who addresses the point says nobody tells him he cannot or should not do it, that everybody says it works, and that the not-starting is down to him (p. 480). What the other passages print is abstention: one inactive distributor does not bring lists, does not sponsor and does not use the products consistently (p. 480); another now calls only people he does not see much of (p. 480). On the author's own account, approaching family and friends is how a distributor finds out which relationships are negative (author's characterisation on p. 474; an active distributor describes the first months of approaching them as the hardest, p. 474). A distributor who does not approach never runs that test, so nothing has happened that would lead to those ties being dropped. On this reading the ambivalence printed on p. 480 is between wanting to start and not starting, not between two sets of advisers, and the kept ties are a trace of the inactivity rather than its cause.

**Its opening.** Because distributors discover which friends and relatives are hostile only by trying to recruit them, an inactive member who never approaches close contacts will retain those friendships as a result of not working the business rather than stalling because outsiders advised against it. Checking whether inactive distributors ever pitched their close circle or heard criticisms from them before putting the business aside would settle whether intact outside ties cause inactivity or merely result from it.

## Reading R07

**The reading.** This group speaks in one passage, so the retelling is short. Asked for their net profit, current and former distributors did two things: they did not produce a clear figure, and they disagreed among themselves about which product margins and which training expenses belong in the sum (p. 463). The paper takes this as a limit on what could be measured and goes on to conclude that money did not sort people (the paper's claim at p. 463). Told from the distributors' side it is a report about them. Among the people doing this work, what the business earns is not a settled number; it depends on what one is prepared to call a cost. The cost in dispute is not a small one. Distributors paid for their training (p. 459), and the training is printed as a system of books, recordings, meetings, rallies and seminars (p. 459); books and tapes are also among the media through which members met model dreams (pp. 465–466). Whoever leaves those expenses out has a business that nets more than whoever puts them in. The wager is that the disagreement in the passage on p. 463 ran between those who stood with [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 462], and that counting the training as a cost of the business, or not counting it, is itself a way of standing toward the organization. If that is so, financial result cannot be laid beside identification as a separate measure, as the passage on p. 463 lays it, since a distributor's own figure already holds an answer to what the training is. This does not dispute that money failed to sort people; it disputes that money was a separate thing to sort them by. The passage does not say along what line the disagreement ran, and it leaves 'could not' and 'would not' unseparated.

**Its opening.** Because a distributor's net profit depends on whether expensive books, tapes, and seminars are counted as business costs, self-reported financial results already reflect a member's allegiance to Amway and cannot serve as an independent variable alongside identification. Recording which informants include training materials and personal product use in their profit calculations across positive, ambivalent, and disidentified groups would show whether accounting rules divide along lines of organizational identification.

## Reading R08

**The reading.** The paper sets the two men side by side as failed support and durable support. The passages print something narrower: what each upline did with a member the pair holds to have been less than fully active. In the passage on p. 479 what the upline offered came through the program: it relied mainly on more meetings. In the passage on p. 480 what the sponsor offered stood apart from the program: the support went on while he repeatedly failed to attend functions, prepare lists, recruit or use the products consistently, and the sponsor used a kinship term for him. Elsewhere what uplines give is printed as running through the work. The researcher's upline tolerated his interviewing so long as he kept selling and trying to recruit (p. 460), and one distributor wanted the Pearl level partly so that he could join golf trips with upline members he liked (p. 471). Read this way, the pair differs in whether the upline's dealings with the member depended on the work, not in how much was given. Where they did, the member left and blames the upline (p. 479). Where they did not, he stayed, values the sponsor, and still does not do the work (p. 480). The second half is the inversion. The tie the paper counts as the strong one is printed beside none of the conduct a strong upline tie is supposed to bring about (the paper's claim at pp. 469–476). On this reading that tie holds because it did not depend on the work; it is what an idle member is left holding, and not a cause of anything he does. The tie in the passage on p. 479 is the ordinary, work-bound one, meeting a man it did not move.

**Its opening.** Because the inactive member's warm sponsor relationship persists while he skips functions, prepares no prospect lists, and recruits no one, that bond appears to survive only because it is detached from business performance rather than acting as a mentoring force that drives encapsulation. Comparing upline relationships that predate Amway as family or friendship ties with purely program-based sponsorships would test whether non-contingent personal bonds enable long-term inactivity while work-bound mentoring drives non-performing recruits to quit in anger.

## Reading R09

**The reading.** Followed through Mark, the session prints three things happening, and a wanted possession for the downline is the slightest of them. First, Mark pursues his own wants: he points out a leather jacket, says he is interested in it himself, and arranges for the clerk to phone the author so that the author can buy it for him when it is reduced (p. 492); he asks about boot shops and goes to one although he knows the author has no interest in boots, and says his wife gets angry when he does this on such outings (p. 493). Second, Mark shows and coaches a manner: he takes control in the first shop, stumps a jeweller with knowledgeable questions (p. 492), tells the two downline that their ignorance of jewellery shows and that they should fake it, praises the author for walking straight in at the next shop, and says [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 493] get treated well (p. 493). Third, Mark works the relationship and the business: he offers to pick up cheaper shorts near his home (p. 492), buys the author a biscuit with a father-and-son joke that the author returns, tells a salesperson the author will soon be rich or else Mark is wasting his time, and begins prospecting that salesperson (p. 493). Against this, the author records declining interest in men's jewellery (p. 492), little success in several clothing shops, being worn out (p. 493), and liking very few things all day, the jacket Mark had pointed out being one of them (p. 492). Mark's own stated view is that most people do not know what they want and learn it from watching the upline want things (p. 493). So in this exhibit what passes to the downline is mainly how Mark carries himself and a familial tie to Mark; the mentor relationship the paper places second is being built inside the practice it places first, and the standard set before the downline is the mentor more than any object. This sits close to what the author concedes on pp. 481–482, that the two kinds of practice blur as other distributors come to shape one's ideal self; it differs in holding that, in the one session printed, the blur is there from the first outing rather than arriving later.

**Its opening.** In the paper's only detailed record of a dream-building trip, the newcomer warms to almost none of the merchandise while the upline shops for himself, coaches an assertive manner, and trades father-son jokes, showing that mentor bonding and role modeling happen inside dream building from the start. Comparing field records of initial dream-building sessions across several sponsorship lines would establish whether those outings mainly attach recruits to specific material possessions or induct them into admiring and imitating their upline mentor.

## Reading R10

**The reading.** Seekership is brought to Amway, not made by it. Members who identify, for or against, arrive already discontented with their job, money or family situation. Dream building gives that discontent a vocabulary rather than breaking a settled sense of self. Those who drift away are those whose outside lives were, or became, satisfactory. On this reading the first branch of the model sorts people by prior circumstance, not by whether a practice succeeded. The paper says identity deficits bite hardest on people in crisis or unhappy with who they are (p. 467). This concedes that prior state conditions the effect. The success-story distributor credits her upline with carrying her through a divorce (p. 470). The trouble predates the bond. Testimonies of an upline helping one member off drugs and feeding a couple in need (p. 471). Again need precedes attachment. In Table 1 the family dream is voiced by a man whose wife is worn out by her job and whose child is in day care all week (p. 465). The dream reads as a description of an existing strain. An ambivalent distributor frames his dream as escaping a job where others control whether he works (p. 480). This is a grievance about present employment. The non-renewer explains leaving by saying her job is now going well and she likes it (pp. 477–478). Deidentification here follows outside contentment. The general model adds a selection box and allows that some people are predisposed to seek, through dissatisfaction or disposition (p. 482). The paper thus names this route itself but does not test it on its own informants. Footnote 9 admits individual differences cannot be ruled out (p. 463). The design did not collect what would rule them out.

**Its opening.** The need for meaning may precede dream building, with the organization supplying an interpretation of difficulties that members already face. Tracing dissatisfaction before entry, during dream building, and after changes in work or family circumstances would distinguish imported seekership from dissatisfaction produced by the organization.

## Reading R11

**The reading.** On a reading centered on engineered wanting, Amway first works on who a distributor is through dream building, before any mentor enters. Newcomers are taught to name a house, a child's lessons, or life without a boss as their own, so that what they lack becomes part of them. The present self, measured against that ideal, is found short—a loser's comfort zone—and the shortfall moves people to act. The push is kept from fading: an attained dream is traded for a larger one, and the most successful are shown starting over. Distributors' discontent is installed, not discovered, and dream building is the chief instrument unsettling the self. Anchors the reading: the paper proposes that Amway manufactures a nonreligious seekership, a discontent about who one is, and names dream building as the primary practice opening that gap — by binding the self to possessions, generating drives from the distance between current and ideal selves, and keeping those drives going (p. 464). Shows the installation step: possessions extend the self-concept, a needs analysis asks what one wants badly enough to do almost anything for, and Table 1 lists the concrete wants — a vast house, swimming lessons for a daughter, no boss — that newcomers learn to claim as their own (pp. 464–465). Delivers the shortfall: a hoped-for possession, once part of the self, reminds its holder of what she lacks and is not; the present self becomes a comfort zone or rut whose occupant is a loser, and the distance between that self and the dream is what drives action (p. 467). Supplies the renewal step: dream building does not end when a dream is reached — stopping is likened to a house left to decay, distributors are told to swap a realized dream for a bigger one and to keep climbing without peaking, and dreams drift toward larger, more abstract aims (p. 468). Confirms the renewal in distributors' own accounts: constant dream building kept members perpetually seeking self-validation, seekers treated dreams as standing motivators, and even the top distributor is described as starting anew all the time (p. 469).

**Its opening.** The reading preserves the explanation through repeatedly created identity shortfalls while adding that the first shortfall arises before mentoring begins. Tracing the earliest dream-building encounters and mentor contacts would establish whether dissatisfaction is produced before, or within, the mentoring relationship.

## Reading R12

**The reading.** The outcome is decided outside Amway, by how a member's existing circle responds when approached. Where family and friends join or at least tolerate the venture, the member's world fills with supporters and looks encapsulated. Where valued others recoil and the member is unwilling to lose them, the member hedges, hides and stalls. Where the member's confidants are hostile, the member turns against the organisation. Encapsulation is then something outsiders do to distributors as much as something positive programming does to members. Non-members told the author they avoid distributor friends and relatives, and a distributor calls the first months the hardest because of how intimates react (p. 474). The barrier is built from both sides. Bringing family and friends in is said to secure [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 473] (p. 473). Who says yes determines the member's surroundings. A friend schedules his wedding against an Amway event, and unsupportive spouses are a named category needing continued persuasion (p. 472). Outsiders are active parties. One ambivalent distributor [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 480] (p. 480). Another disowns Amway on the spot when a co-worker repeats a slur about it. The stalling follows an outsider's reaction. Ambivalent members restrict their calls to acquaintances outside daily life and agonise about exploiting close ties (p. 481). Footnote 16 has the author's own enthusiasm sagging around critical colleagues. Disidentified former members made sense of their experience with confidants who were firmly negative (p. 479). The valence of the outcome matches the valence of the audience.

**Its opening.** The social surroundings that sustain or disrupt identification may depend on how existing contacts respond to recruitment. Following comparable recruitment encounters through outsiders' responses, subsequent contact, and changes in attachment would distinguish externally produced barriers from those produced by positive programming.

## Reading R13

**The reading.** Read the ruling-out as provisional. The paper prints that no current or former distributor interviewed gave a clear answer on net profit or loss (p. 463), and that informants disagreed on whether tapes, books and functions count as costs (p. 463). The one financial marker it names for the people it calls successful is [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 463] (p. 463). What the two observations on p. 463 establish, then, is that identification type did not follow sales volume. Each informant's net position, and which way money moved between that informant and the people above them, is not observed for anyone. That unobserved quantity is what the printed disidentification material is about. One disidentified informant describes paying about $120 at his recruitment as the way out of a pressured sale (p. 479). The author's own summary of how disidentified distributors re-read upline warmth is that upline were being pleasant in order to make money for themselves (author's characterisation on p. 479). Members pay a fee of about $[7 words of Pratt (2000), p. 459] (p. 459). An upline tells a salesperson in the author's hearing that the author will be rich soon or the upline is wasting his time (p. 493), and the author reports that those above him cared only that he was selling and trying to sponsor (p. 460 n. 5). The wager is that the ending could go the other way on a narrower question: whether a member comes out as someone money flows from or someone money flows to may sort with the direction of their identification, and the comparison on p. 463 could not have detected it.

**Its opening.** A comparison based on sales volume leaves open whether net financial position distinguishes different forms of identification. Constructing comparable accounts of receipts, fees, training expenses, and payments between sponsorship levels for each identification group would test that financial distinction.

## Reading R14

**The reading.** The concept is doing two jobs. X is a stated current dissatisfaction with one's own life; Y is excitement about a dream with no such dissatisfaction stated. The printed passages let them come apart. The author's own journal reports being caught up in a dream of personal freedom while also thinking the dreams foolish, and names nothing wrong with his present life (p. 466). A former distributor says they were excited at the beginning about their own business and financial independence and then lost interest (p. 477): Y occurred and lapsed, and X is not reported. The distributor who did not renew gives her reason as a present life she likes, a job that has reached the point of little overtime (p. 477): X is absent, and nothing is said about dreams. Where X is printed, the speaker ties it to paid work or to things lacked: a wife burned out at her job, two working parents and [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 465] (p. 465); not wanting to be at the mercy of people at work (p. 480); not having what the recruiter had (p. 478). The wager is that X, not Y, is what goes with an identification of any kind forming, and that the printed passages show Y as what sessions, tapes and displays deliver while showing X in speakers' accounts of their jobs and households. If so, 'sensebreaking failed' on p. 477 covers two different things: excitement that was produced and faded, and a present life that was never, or no longer, found wanting.

**Its opening.** The first branching point may combine two observations that need separate treatment: wanting a future possibility and finding one's present life inadequate. Coding each informant separately for current dissatisfaction, dream excitement, and later identification would show which condition distinguishes attachment from detachment.

## Reading R15

**The reading.** The pair says the opposed leaver kept the wish for change and the detached one lost it. The passages on the opposed side do not line up that way. One former distributor was drawn by a successful member's large home because he lacked such possessions (p. 478) and describes becoming a lawyer as a dream worth hard work (p. 478): the wish is printed for him, and nothing about his upline is. The former distributor in the passage on p. 479 blames his upline for not recognizing the sales ability he came with and for relying on more meetings instead of finding a way to motivate him: no wish he was pursuing is printed, only a man who by his own telling still needed motivating. A third, whom the author files under the same opposed outcome, says he bought in only to get out of an embarrassing situation at a high-pressure presentation (p. 479); no passage gives him a dream. No passage says that the man of the passages on p. 478 is the man of the passage on p. 479. The upline tie does not separate the two sides either. By the author's account the detached formed no strong bonds with distributors (p. 478), and those who came to disidentify are reported with sparse and distant upline relationships (p. 479). The paper puts that weak tie after the loss of desire on the one side (p. 478) and before the opposition on the other (p. 479, the author's account); this reading puts it after on both. What the passages on p. 479 print, and the passage on pp. 477–478 does not, is a wrong: something done to the speaker by the people who brought him in or stood above him. The passage on pp. 477–478 has no Amway person in it; what changed was her regular job. The wager is that a leaver ends opposed when he carries such a wrong out with him, whether or not the wish for change survived, and ends detached when he carries none.

**Its opening.** The contrast between detached and oppositional exits may turn on an enduring grievance even when both involve weak mentor ties. Reconstructing each leaver's aspirations, perceived mistreatment, withdrawal from mentors, and eventual stance in time order would establish whether grievance distinguishes the two exits.

## Reading R16

**The reading.** There is one practice, not two. The upline relationship does all the work, and dream building is something done inside it. Dreams are supplied, shaped and kept alive by the mentor, so a 'successful' dream is evidence of a working bond and a faded dream is evidence of a bond that never formed or went sour. The paper's two-by-two logic then collapses into a single dimension, the quality of the tie to the upline, with ambivalence as its middle value. Members learn what to dream mainly by modelling [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 465] people in their own line (p. 465). The content of the dream comes from the upline. Upline and downline jointly craft the individual dream from a finite approved menu (p. 466). Dream building is described as a relational activity. In the appendix the Emerald chooses the shops, the colours and the items, and the author likes almost nothing all day (pp. 492–493). Meanwhile a joking father-son exchange occurs. The session produces a bond more visibly than a want. A distributor wants to reach Pearl in order to golf with his upline because he likes them (p. 471). The dream's object is the relationship. An interviewee says those who stay active are those who keep [7 words of Pratt (2000), p. 475] (p. 475). This is a member's own single-factor theory. The disidentified former distributor complains that his upline did not know how to excite him (p. 479). Failure to kindle the dream and failure of the bond are the same event in his telling. The deidentified leaver describes growing distance from his sponsors and discomfort with reverence for Diamonds (p. 478). The case filed under failed sensebreaking carries a relationship story. The paper acknowledges that the two kinds of tactic blur once sensegiving starts, as the ideal self comes under other distributors' influence (pp. 481–482).

**Its opening.** The explanation through two practices may reduce to one relationship process if dream creation and loss follow the quality of the mentor bond. Following changes in mentor bonds, dreams, and identification within the same members would show whether aspirations vary independently of the relationship.

## Reading R17

**The reading.** The paper sets money aside too quickly. What it shows is not that finances are irrelevant but that members cannot or will not reckon them, and that the unreckoned items are precisely what flows up the line: tapes, books, functions, fees. Read this way, positive identification is the state of not having done the sum. Disidentification is the state of having done it and concluded that the warmth was commercial. Inactivity is the cheap holding position of paying only the renewal. The identification types are stances toward a ledger. No current or former distributor could, or would, state net profit, and they disagreed on whether training materials count as costs (p. 463). The paper reports this as a surprise and moves on. Here it is the central finding. The company's own figure for average monthly gross income of an active distributor is very low, and footnote 8 says nearly all actives fall short of high volume (p. 463). Most members are spending, not earning. Members pay a joining fee and often pay for books and tapes (p. 459). The costs are structural. Mentors direct which books to read and tapes to buy (p. 471). The sensegiver is also the person steering purchases. Disidentified members reinterpret upline kindness as a means to the upline's own income and feel used (p. 479). Their turn is an economic re-reading of the relationship. One former member recalls paying the entry sum as [6 words of Pratt (2000), p. 479] pressured pitch (p. 479). His grievance begins with a payment. Inactive distributors are defined as those who renew annually but do little else (p. 461).
