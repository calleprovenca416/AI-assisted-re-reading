# Linking rules, version 2.1

*3 October 2026. **Frozen on 3 October, after a third review and a fourth
check;** the hash and the seed are in `link_rules_v2_1_freeze.json`. It amends
version 2 (`LINK_RULES_v2.md`), which was frozen on 30 September and sent to no
session. A colleague then reviewed version 2 with the paper
(`LINK_RULES_v2_colleague_review.docx`), and on 1–3 October five of her points
were adopted. Version 2.1 makes those changes, listed at the end, before any
marker, passage or pair exists. It supersedes version 1.1 (`LINK_RULES_v1_1.md`)
for the next test. Version 1.1 did not pass the test of 29–30 September, and the
cause was judgment 1, what a stub shows (`LINK_TEST_RESULT.md`). Who may receive
this file: nobody (—). Part S is sent word for word to the marker session, Part
P to the passage session, and Part A to the coders, each inside its own sender
file. Part B is never sent.*

Version 1.1 split brief v2's one question into separate judgments, and derived
the code from them by a fixed table. The question was whether a fragment bears on
a claim, and which way. The test of 29–30 September showed that the judgments
about each claim, and about how the paper uses a passage, held. Judgment 1, what
the stub shows of a claim's condition and outcome, did not. The stubs report
concrete events and the claims name abstract conditions and outcomes. Nothing
said when a concrete event shows an abstract outcome, and the two models parted
exactly there.

Version 2 changed judgment 1 and what it rests on. Version 2.1 adds the last of
these:

- **Markers.** Each claim gets observable signs of its condition and of its
  outcome, called markers. A separate session writes them once, from the paper's
  own definitions and descriptions, before any coder sees a pair (Part S). A
  passage shows a condition or an outcome only by showing one of its markers.
- **The passage, not the stub.** Judgment 1 is made from the passage in the
  paper. Another separate session fixes where each passage starts and ends,
  before any coder sees a pair (Part P). The stub only helps to find it.
- **Names.** A passage that only names the condition or the outcome is marked
  `named`. Every result is computed with names counted and again without.
- **A third kind of passage, `pattern`.** It covers what the paper reports as
  recurring across the cases observed, even when the author also explains it.
  Every result is computed with patterns counted as reports and again as
  readings.
- **Practices.** A claim's condition is worded as the claim words it. Where it is
  a practice working, each of its markers is tagged as a sign of the practice
  working, or of it only being carried out. Every result is computed with
  carried-out markers counted and again without.

Markers are written for a claim's condition, and a `maps` claim's markers depend
on its form. So the marker session also records each claim's condition and form,
under version 1.1's rules for them. Coders take all three as given. Judgment 3,
how the paper uses a passage, is unchanged. So are the author link and the
derivation table.

## Part S — the claim specification, as the marker session receives it

### What you are looking at

You have a published qualitative research paper and a table of some of its
**claims**: things the paper asserts, in its own voice, about how something
works. Each claim is printed with its statement and the page or pages where the
paper makes it. It also has its **outcome**, which is what the claim says gets
affected, or `none`. And it has its **asserted direction**, which is which way
the claim says the outcome goes. Someone else wrote these. Take them as given.

For each claim you record three things: its **condition**, its **form** and its
**markers**. Other people will later read passages of the paper and judge, for
each claim, whether a passage shows its condition or its outcome. They will judge
that only by your markers, so your markers decide what counts. Write the markers
the paper supports, no more and no fewer. A missing marker hides evidence the
paper prints, and a loose one counts what it should not.

### Four rules

1. **Only this paper.** Do not search the web. If you recognise the paper or its
   author, set aside everything you know from elsewhere.
2. **Code what the definitions say.** Do not ask what any answer is used for
   beyond what this file tells you. Knowing which answer leads where can tilt a
   borderline call without your noticing.
3. **Write the reason before you choose the code.** It is easy to pick a code and
   then justify it; the reverse is harder and more honest.
4. **When a case is genuinely unclear,** give your best answer and add `| unclear`
   at the end of its line.

### Each claim: its condition and its form

For every claim in the table, record its condition and its form. Work from the
claim's statement, outcome and asserted direction as printed. Where they seem not
to fit together, go by the printed outcome and asserted direction, and say so in
your reason.

**Condition.** The state that the printed asserted direction says brings the
outcome about, in a short noun phrase worded as that state. For *the more people
must sign off, the longer the decision takes*, the condition is *many people
having to sign off*. For *when the routine fails, mistakes reach the table*, it is
*the routine failing*. If the printed outcome is `none`, the condition is `none`.

**A practice as the condition.** A practice here is anything people or an
organisation do in a regular way, whatever the paper calls it: a practice, a
process, a routine, a technique or a programme. Word the condition as the claim
words it. If the claim says that a practice brings the outcome about (*tasting at
both stations keeps dishes from coming back*), the condition is the practice
being carried out.
If the claim says that the practice working, succeeding or failing brings the
outcome about (*when the tasting routine works, dishes go out right*), the
condition is that. If the claim's statement and its printed direction word this
differently, go by the printed direction, and say so in your reason.

**Form.** What kind of relation the claim asserts. Choose one.

| form | the claim says | an invented example |
|---|---|---|
| `produces` | the condition raises, lowers or brings about the outcome: "the more X, the more Y", "when X, then Y". A claim about what happens when something fails is `produces`, with the failure as its condition | *Time pressure makes cooks skip the tasting step.* |
| `requires` | the outcome cannot happen without the condition: "only when", "only if", "cannot without" | *A dish leaves the kitchen only after the head chef has tasted it.* |
| `maps` | the claim sorts cases by a condition and gives a different kind of outcome for each | *Cooks trained in hotels become sticklers for routine; cooks trained in family restaurants become improvisers.* |
| `none` | the condition makes no systematic difference to the outcome | *How long a cook has worked in the kitchen makes no difference to how often they skip the tasting step.* |
| `describes` | the printed outcome is `none`, and only then: the claim defines a term, describes a practice, a process, a setting or a kind, or says how the research was done | *The kitchen runs a three-stage tasting routine.* |

A claim whose printed outcome is `none` is always `describes`, and a claim with a
printed outcome never is, even a claim about how the research was done. Code the
relation the claim asserts, not how firmly it asserts it. If a claim asserts more
than one relation, code the one whose outcome is the printed outcome, and say so
in your reason. For a `maps` claim, the condition is what the claim sorts cases
by: *where the cook trained*.

### Each claim: its markers

A **marker** is an observable sign that the condition holds, or that the outcome
has occurred. It is something a person did, said, had or felt, or something that
happened or was the case, that a passage of the paper could report. For the
condition and for the outcome, write each separate sign the paper gives, however
many there are; most need one to four. Give each in one short line in your own
words. After it, give the page and the first few words of the sentence it comes
from. Number them `c1`, `c2`, and so on for the condition, and `o1`, `o2`, and
so on for the outcome.

- **Where markers come from.** They come from where the paper defines the
  condition or the outcome, or describes in general terms what it involves or how
  it shows. They do not come from a single event, quotation or case that the paper
  presents as an instance of it. A marker says what any instance would show, not
  what one instance showed.
- **Each marker must be enough on its own.** A passage that shows any one marker
  will count as showing the condition or the outcome. List only signs that would
  each be enough by themselves. Where the condition or the outcome can show in
  several ways, each way can be a marker. Where it joins two things, such as
  "both X and Y" or "X while Y fails", each marker must be a sign of both
  together. A sign of one part alone is not a marker.
- **What a marker is not.** A marker is not an abstract term for the condition or
  the outcome: its name, a word for it, or another of the paper's abstract terms.
  It is not one of the author's categories applied to people or events. Nor is it
  a sign of many things at once rather than of this one. Words that describe an
  act or a state anyone could see are never a name, even when they are the claim's
  own words.
- **None printed.** If the paper gives no observable sign of the condition, or of
  the outcome, write `none printed` for it, and list the pages you searched. Do not
  supply a sign from elsewhere.
- **Keep the condition and the outcome apart.** No sign may appear under both,
  and no single event may be a sign of one and the contrary of a sign of the
  other. A sign of the outcome the claim names is never a marker of its
  condition.
- **A practice working.** Where the condition is a practice working or
  succeeding, list two kinds of marker, and tag each one in brackets after it:
  - `working`: a sign that the practice did what the paper says it does when it
    works, short of the outcome the claim names;
  - `carried out`: a sign only that the practice was done.

  List the `carried out` signs as well as the `working` ones. They are the one
  exception to the rule that each marker must be enough on its own: a sign that
  the practice was carried out is not a sign that it worked. If the paper gives no
  sign of the practice working apart from the claim's outcome, say so in your
  reason, and give the `carried out` markers alone.

  Where the condition joins practices, or joins a practice with something else,
  each marker still shows the whole condition. Tag it `working` only if it shows
  every practice that the condition says is working, working. A `maps` claim's
  categories take no `carried out` markers and no tags: give only signs that tell
  one category from another. Where the condition is a practice failing, its
  markers are signs of it failing, as the paper describes that, apart from the
  outcome. Where the condition is a practice being carried out, its markers are
  signs of that. Neither of these last two needs a tag.
- **Direction.** Write the markers for the condition holding and for the outcome
  occurring, as the printed direction pins them. The people who judge passages
  also code when a condition does not hold and when an outcome goes the other
  way. They do that with the same markers, so you need not list the opposites.
- **`maps`.** Number the categories the claim sorts cases by: `k1`, `k2`, and so
  on. For each category, give its markers (`k1c1`, …), as for a condition. Then
  name the kind of outcome the claim gives for it, with its markers (`k1o1`, …),
  as for an outcome. Take which kind goes with which category from the claim's
  statement and printed direction. If neither says, write `no mapping given`,
  and give the markers of the categories only. If the paper defines the kinds of
  outcome by the categories themselves,
  so that no sign of a category could differ from a sign of its kind of outcome,
  write `none printed` for the kinds of outcome, and say so in your reason.
- **`none`.** Markers for the condition and the outcome, as for `produces`.
- **`describes`.** The condition is `none`. Give the markers of an instance of
  what the claim describes, as for an outcome, numbered `o1`, `o2`, and so on.

Invented examples, with invented pages:

- *Time pressure makes cooks skip the tasting step.* Condition: *time pressure*.
  Form: `produces`.
  - `c1` orders waiting past the time promised to the diner (p. 7, "By seven the
    tickets…")
  - `c2` a cook told to hurry (p. 7, "By seven the tickets…")
  - `o1` a dish leaving the station untasted (p. 8, "To skip the taste…")
  - `o2` a cook saying they did not taste a dish (p. 8, "To skip the taste…")
  - Not markers:
    - "pressure on the line" is the condition's name in other words;
    - "a harried cook" is one of the author's categories;
    - "a stressed cook" is a sign of many things;
    - "the cook who skipped the sauce on the night of the wedding party" is one
      instance, not a sign.
- *When the tasting routine works, dishes go out right.* Condition: *the tasting
  routine working*. Form: `produces`. Opening words are left out below.
  - `c1` a taster catching a fault in a dish before it leaves the kitchen
    (working) (p. 9)
  - `c2` a dish tasted at both stations before it leaves (carried out) (p. 9)
  - `o1` no diner sending the dish back (p. 10)
  - `o2` a diner praising the dish (p. 10)
  - The claim is about the routine working, so each condition marker has a tag.
    Tasting at both stations shows the routine carried out, not the routine
    working. Neither marker is a sign of the dishes going out right.
- *Tasting at both stations keeps dishes from coming back.* Condition: *dishes
  being tasted at both stations*, the practice carried out, as the claim words
  it. Form: `produces`.
  - `c1` a dish tasted at both stations before it leaves (p. 9)
  - `o1` no diner sending the dish back (p. 10)
  - No tags: the claim is about the practice being carried out.
- *When a strict head chef runs the tasting routine, dishes go out right.* The
  condition joins two things, so each marker is a sign of both: `c1` a head chef
  who tastes every dish herself, in a kitchen that runs the routine (p. 11).
- *When the tasting routine works under a strict head chef, dishes go out right.*
  The condition joins a practice working with something else. Each marker shows
  both, and is tagged by what it shows of the practice:
  - `c1` a head chef who tastes every dish herself, in a kitchen where a taster
    caught a fault before a dish left (working) (p. 11)
  - `c2` a head chef who tastes every dish herself, in a kitchen that tastes at
    both stations (carried out) (p. 11)
- *Cooks trained in hotels become sticklers for routine; cooks trained in family
  restaurants become improvisers.* Condition: *where the cook trained*. Form:
  `maps`.
  - `k1` trained in a hotel kitchen: `k1c1` an earlier job in a hotel kitchen
    (p. 12). Kind of outcome: a stickler for routine, with `k1o1` following the
    written recipe exactly (p. 13) and `k1o2` objecting when another cook skips a
    step (p. 13).
  - `k2` trained in a family restaurant: `k2c1` an earlier job in a family
    restaurant (p. 12). Kind of outcome: an improviser, with `k2o1` changing a
    dish without asking (p. 14) and `k2o2` cooking without a recipe card (p. 14).
- *The kitchen runs a three-stage tasting routine.* Condition: `none`. Form:
  `describes`.
  - `o1` a dish tasted at the station, at the pass and by the head chef (p. 5)
- *A calm kitchen breeds loyalty among its cooks*, in a paper that never says what
  loyalty looks like. The outcome's markers are `none printed`, with the pages
  searched.

*(End of Part S.)*

## Part P — the passages, as the passage session receives them

### What you are looking at

You have a published qualitative research paper and a list of **fragments**. A
fragment is a piece of evidence the paper prints, such as an event, a quotation,
an observation or a table cell. Each has a label such as K07, a page, and a short
**stub** that someone else wrote to summarise it.

For each fragment, find the passage in the paper that the stub summarises, and
record where it starts and where it ends. Other people will later judge what each
passage shows. They will read exactly the words you mark, no more and no fewer. A
passage that stops short hides what the paper prints, and one that runs on adds
what does not belong to it.

### Four rules

1. **Only this paper.** Do not search the web. If you recognise the paper or its
   author, set aside everything you know from elsewhere.
2. **Follow what the definitions say.** Do not ask what any answer is used for
   beyond what this file tells you. Knowing which answer leads where can tilt a
   borderline call without your noticing.
3. **Write the reason first.** Say what the passage reports, then give its first
   and last words.
4. **When a case is genuinely unclear,** give your best answer and add `| unclear`
   at the end of its line.

### The passage

The passage is what the stub summarises, as the paper prints it. It is one of
three things.

- **An occurrence.** If the stub summarises one occurrence, the passage is every
  sentence that reports it: what happened, what was done, said or written, or what
  was the case on that occasion.
- **A recurrence.** If the stub summarises what recurred across cases, the passage
  is every sentence that states the recurrence. An instance the paper gives of it,
  such as a quotation or an episode, is an occurrence of its own. It stays out if
  it is a fragment of its own in your list, or if the stub does not report it.
- **The author's own statement.** If the stub summarises a statement of the
  author's own, such as an explanation, an interpretation, a claim about how
  things work or the author's framework, the passage is the sentences that make
  that statement. Do not swap in a report nearby.

For an occurrence or a recurrence:

- the passage runs from the first such sentence to the last, with everything in
  between;
- it includes a quotation that belongs to the occurrence, with the words that
  introduce it;
- it includes a sentence that reports a fact of the occurrence even if the stub
  leaves that fact out, such as a number;
- it leaves out a sentence before or after that only comments on the occurrence:
  one that says why it happened, what it meant, or how it bears on the paper's
  argument. A comment inside a sentence that also reports the occurrence stays
  in;
- it leaves out other occurrences, even in the same paragraph.

For any passage:

- a table cell, a figure or a footnote is its own passage. Give the table's or
  figure's number, and the cell's row and column;
- if the passage runs across a page break, give both pages.

### What you record

For each fragment, record:

- its label, and the page or pages of the passage;
- the passage's first five words and its last five words, exactly as printed,
  punctuation included;
- a reason of one line: what the passage reports, and anything the stub reports
  that the passage does not print.

If the passage is ten words or fewer, give it whole. If its first or last five
words occur more than once on the page, give more words until they occur only
once. If you cannot find the passage, write `not found` and say what you looked
for.

Invented examples:

- **An occurrence.** The stub reads: *During the rush a cook sent a sauce out
  untasted, saying there was no time.* The paper reads: *"The rush began at
  seven. By eight every ticket was overdue. Mia sent the béarnaise out without
  tasting it. 'There was no time,' she told me later. Such moments show how
  routines give way under pressure. The next evening was quiet."* The passage
  runs from "The rush began at seven." to "time,' she told me later." It leaves
  out the comment that follows, and the next evening, which is another occasion.
- **A recurrence.** K12's stub reads: *Several cooks said they skipped tasting
  when tickets piled up.* The paper reads: *"Several cooks told me they skipped
  tasting when tickets piled up. Joe put it bluntly: 'When the rail is full, the
  spoon stays in the drawer.'"* Joe's words are K13 in the list. So K12's passage
  is the first sentence alone.
- **The author's own statement.** The stub reads: *Tasting routines give way when
  time runs short.* The paper reads: *"These episodes suggest that tasting
  routines give way when time runs short. The same may hold for other checks."*
  The passage is the first sentence. The episodes it refers to are other
  passages, and the second sentence is a further statement the stub does not
  summarise.

*(End of Part P.)*

## Part A — the pair judgments, as coders receive them

### What you are looking at

You have a published qualitative research paper and a table drawn from it. The
table lists some of the paper's **claims**: things the paper asserts, in its own
voice, about how something works. Each claim is printed with:

- its statement, and the page or pages where the paper makes it;
- its **outcome**: what the claim says gets affected, or `none`;
- its **asserted direction**: which way the claim says the outcome goes;
- its **condition**: the state that the asserted direction says brings the
  outcome about;
- its **form**: the kind of relation it asserts (below);
- its **markers**: observable signs of the condition (`c1`, `c2`, …) and of the
  outcome (`o1`, `o2`, …). Each has the page where the paper defines or describes
  it, or is `none printed` where the paper gives no sign. For some claims, each
  condition marker also carries a tag, `working` or `carried out` (below).

The table also lists **pairs**. Each pair joins one claim to one **fragment**: a
piece of evidence the paper prints, such as an event, a quotation, an observation
or a table cell. Each fragment has a label such as K07, a page, the first and
last words of its **passage** in the paper, the passage's text, and a short
**stub** that summarises it. One fragment can appear in several pairs. The stub
is there to help you find the passage. It may leave things out.

Someone else wrote the claims, their conditions, forms and markers, the passages'
first and last words, and the stubs. Take them as given, and make the judgments
the rules below ask for. If you think a claim's condition, form or markers are
wrong, say so once, in a note under that claim, and code by them as given all the
same.

The pairs were drawn from the paper for this exercise. Some join a passage and a
claim that the paper connects, and some do not. Do not assume either.

### Four rules

1. **Only this paper.** Do not search the web. If you recognise the paper or its
   author, set aside everything you know from elsewhere.
2. **Code what the definitions say.** Do not ask what any answer is used for.
   Knowing which answer leads where can tilt a borderline call without your
   noticing.
3. **Write the reason before you choose the code.** It is easy to pick a code and
   then justify it; the reverse is harder and more honest.
4. **When a case is genuinely unclear,** give your best answer and add `| unclear`
   at the end of its line.

### The forms

| form | the claim says |
|---|---|
| `produces` | the condition raises, lowers or brings about the outcome |
| `requires` | the outcome cannot happen without the condition |
| `maps` | the claim sorts cases by a condition and gives a different kind of outcome for each. The table numbers the categories `k1`, `k2`, and so on, each with its markers and with the kind of outcome the claim gives for it |
| `none` | the condition makes no systematic difference to the outcome |
| `describes` | the claim defines a term, describes a practice, a process, a setting or a kind, or says how the research was done. It has no condition, and its markers are signs of an instance of what it describes |

### Each pair: three judgments

For every pair you make three judgments, all about **the passage**. Make judgment
1 for every pair before you make judgments 2 and 3 for any.

**Finding the passage.** The passage is every word from its first words to its
last, as the table gives them. Nothing before its first words or after its last
words is part of it. The table also prints the passage's text. If the print and
the paper differ, go by the paper. Where the stub and the passage differ, judge
the passage.

If you cannot find the passage, write `not found` in place of that pair's codes,
in judgment 1 and again in judgments 2 and 3. Say in the reason what you looked
for.

**1. What does the passage show?** From the passage, against the claim's markers.

Judge from the passage alone. Do not judge from the stub, from the words around
the passage, from other passages, from what you know about how organisations
work, or from an inference. You may read back before the passage only to find
out who or which thing its words refer to. Nothing else said of them there
counts: only what the passage itself reports shows a marker, and only a name the
passage itself uses counts.

**Markers decide.** The passage shows the condition, or the outcome, only if it
shows one of its markers. One marker is enough, and the passage need not use the
marker's words. Where the table says `none printed`, no marker can show that
part. Code it `not shown`, unless a name shows it (below).

**No further step.** Ask whether you could say what the passage reports in the
marker's words without adding a fact, a cause, a motive or a meaning. If you
could, the passage shows the marker. If you would have to add something, it does
not. Showing part of a marker is not showing it: a marker that joins two things is
shown only if the passage shows both.

Your reason names every marker you found, by its label, such as `c2` or `o1`,
and quotes the passage's words that show it, a few words at most. If you found
none, say so.

**What the passage reports, not what the author makes of it.** Count only what the
passage reports: what happened, what was done, said or written, what was the
case, or what recurred across cases. A fact the passage reports counts wherever it
sits in the sentence, even inside a "because" clause.

- **The author's explanation never counts:** why something happened, what it
  meant, and any link the author draws between facts. A cause the author only
  suggests is not a reported fact.
- **What people in the setting said, wrote, thought or felt counts,** including
  the reasons they gave. A thought or feeling the passage reports them having
  counts, even when the passage quotes no one.
- **Keep the mode.** A passage shows a marker only in the mode it reports.
  Something wanted, planned, advised or ordered is not something done. An order
  shows the order, not that it was followed. A wish shows the wish, not that it
  came true. A fact a person gives as their reason counts as a reported fact.
  That it was the cause counts only under other cause.

**Showing and naming.** Words show a marker when they report something that
matches it: an act, a state, an event, or what someone said. They count whoever's
words they are. Words that describe an act or a state anyone could see are never a
name, even when they are the claim's own words ("skips the tasting step"). Words
only name the condition or the outcome when they apply an abstract term that
stands for it, instead of reporting what happened. Such a term is the claim's name
for it, a word for it, another of the paper's abstract terms, or one of the
author's categories applied to people or events ("the cooks the author calls
harried"). A marker is never such a term, so ask: would the words that show it be
allowed as a marker?

- If only a name shows the condition or the outcome, code it as shown and add
  `named`. Quote the name in your reason. If a marker shows it too, do not add
  `named`. Where a condition joins two things, and the passage shows one of them
  as a marker gives it and only names the other, code it as shown and add
  `named`. Where the markers are tagged, that marker must be a `working` one.
- A name can also show the condition not holding, or the outcome going the other
  way. A name for a contrary state ("the cooks the author calls unhurried", for
  time pressure) is `absent, named` or `other way, named`.
- A name counts only when it is applied to what happened. A name that appears only
  in the author's explanation of something else is set aside with the
  explanation.

| observation | code |
|---|---|
| **condition** | `present`: the passage shows a marker of the condition holding. `absent`: the passage shows the condition not holding: it reports something that rules the condition out, usually the contrary of a marker. `not shown`: neither. The contrary of one marker is not enough while another marker could still hold. Add `named` to `present` or `absent` when only a name shows it |
| **outcome** | `as asserted`: the passage shows a marker of the outcome, going the way the asserted direction says. `other way`: the passage shows the outcome going the opposite way, or not occurring: it reports something that rules out the outcome as asserted, usually the contrary of a marker. `not shown`: neither. The contrary of one marker is not enough while another marker could still hold. Add `named` to `as asserted` or `other way` when only a name shows it |
| **other cause** | `yes`: the passage reports that something other than the claim's condition brought the outcome about, in one of two ways only: a person in the setting gave it as the reason, or the passage reports an order, a rule or an instruction that called for the outcome. `no` otherwise, including when the author ties the outcome to another cause, and when the reason a person gives is the claim's own condition in their words, whether or not it shows a marker |
| **bounded** | `yes`: the passage shows the claim holding in one circumstance and not in another, where the difference is something the claim's statement does not mention, such as a kind of person, a setting or a time. `no` otherwise, including a passage that shows only a case where the claim fails |

An absence you would have to infer does not count. Code `absent` only if the
passage shows the condition not holding, and `present` only if it shows the
condition holding. Code the outcome against the asserted direction as printed,
whatever the condition. If the claim says the outcome rises and the passage shows
it falling, that is `other way`, even when the passage also shows the condition
absent. Keep the outcome's polarity as it is; do not re-sign it.

**Both sides.** If the passage shows cases on both sides of the claim's own
condition, code the side where the condition held, and say "both sides" in your
reason. For a `requires` claim, code instead the side where the outcome occurred.
If it occurred on both sides, code the side without the condition; if on
neither, the side with it. This is not `bounded`, which is about a difference the
claim does not mention.

**The same case.** Code the condition and the outcome of one case: the people or
the thing that the passage reports each of. A group and one of its members are
the same case. Where the passage reports more than one case, take the first of
these that applies, and say "different cases" in your reason:

1. the condition is shown holding in one case and not holding in another: "both
   sides" applies, above;
2. the outcome is reported of one case only: code that case, with the condition
   as the passage shows it for that case, `not shown` if it does not;
3. the outcome is reported of several cases, and the condition is shown for one
   of them only: code that one;
4. the outcome is reported of several cases, going different ways, and the
   condition is shown the same way for each: if the pair is `bounded`, code the
   case where the claim holds; otherwise code the outcome `not shown`;
5. the outcome is reported of several cases, going different ways, and the
   condition is shown for none of them: code the condition and the outcome
   `not shown`, and `bounded` `no`.

A passage that shows the claim holding for some and not for others, where the
difference is something the claim does not mention, is `bounded`, as the table
above says.

**One fact, one role.** If the only thing the passage shows matches both a marker
of the condition and a marker of the outcome, code it as the outcome, and code the
condition `not shown`. Code the condition `present` or `absent` only when the
passage shows it apart from the outcome. When the condition is a practice working
or failing, judge it from what the passage shows of the practice itself, never
from whether the outcome came about.

**A practice working.** Some claims' condition markers carry a tag, `working` or
`carried out`. The condition of such a claim is a practice working or
succeeding, alone or joined with something else. A `working` marker is a sign of
that. A `carried out` marker is a sign only that the practice was done. Check
every marker of such a condition, and name each one the passage shows. Then code
the condition by the first of these that applies:

1. the passage shows a `working` marker: `present`;
2. the passage rules the condition out: `absent`. It does so when it shows the
   contrary of every `working` marker, or the practice not carried out at all,
   or, where the condition joins several things, any one of them not holding;
3. it takes a name to show whether the condition holds: `present, named` if the
   name gives the whole condition holding, alone or with what the passage shows
   of its other parts; `absent, named` if it names a state that rules the
   condition out;
4. the passage shows a `carried out` marker: `present`;
5. otherwise: `not shown`.

- If the table lists no `working` marker, nothing can show the contrary of one.

  carried out at all count in step 2.
- Step 3 comes before step 4, so a name decides even when a `carried out` marker
  is shown too. Name that marker in your reason all the same.
- A name never completes a `carried out` marker. Step 4 needs the whole marker
  shown.

## Part B — how the codes are derived (never sent)

### The specification and the passages

The claim's condition, form and markers come from the marker session (Part S),
and each passage's first and last words from the passage session (Part P). Both
are the same for both coders. The marker session sees the claims only, and the
passage session the fragments only; neither sees a pair. Both are frozen after
the rules and before the sample is drawn. A fragment the passage session cannot
find leaves the pool the sample is drawn from, and is listed.

Before the freeze, both are read in full, and only a clear breach of Part S or
Part P may be fixed, such as:

- a marker that is a name, a word for the condition or the outcome, one of the
  author's categories, or one instance;
- a sign listed under both the condition and the outcome;
- a marker of a joint condition that shows only one part;
- a wrong tag: `working` on a marker that shows only the practice carried out or
  shows the claim's outcome, or `carried out` on one that shows the practice
  working;
- a passage that takes in another occurrence, or stops before a sentence that
  reports its own.

Each fix is logged with its reason. Nobody adds, drops or rewords a marker, or
moves a passage, to fit a pair.

### Three kinds of link

| layer | a pair belongs to it when | what it records |
|---|---|---|
| **author link** | use is `evidence` or `qualification`, whatever the kind | the paper's own connection between a passage and its claim |
| **statement** | use is `restatement` | where the paper states the claim; never evidence for it |
| **evidence link** | kind is `report`, or `pattern` when patterns count as reports (below); use is not `restatement`; and the table below gives a code other than no link (the evidence test) | printed evidence bearing on the claim, whether or not the paper draws the connection |

A pair can be both an author link and an evidence link, or either alone. An author
link on a `reading` records that the paper leans on its own interpretation; it is
never an evidence link.

### Names, patterns and practices, both ways

- **Names.** An observation marked `named` counts as shown when names are
  counted. When names are not counted, it is read as `not shown`, before anything
  else. The one exception is a condition with tagged markers, which the
  Practices rule below reads.
- **Patterns.** *Patterns as reports* puts patterns where version 1.1 put them,
  among the reports. *Patterns as readings* never makes a pattern an evidence
  link. The kind code itself is compared on its three values. A `none` claim needs
  evidence across cases, so with patterns as readings it can show nothing. That
  is the treatment's doing, not the paper's.
- **Practices.** Where a condition's markers are tagged, the script reads the
  markers each coder listed as shown. The reply format asks for that list apart
  from the reason, because a reason can also mention a marker that is not shown.
  A `present` counts as shown if the coder listed a `working` marker; or listed a
  `carried out` marker, when carried-out markers are counted; or marked it
  `named`, when names are counted. Otherwise it is read as `not shown`. An
  `absent` stays as coded, unless it is `named` and names are not counted. Then
  it is read without the name: as `present` if the coder listed a `carried out`
  marker and those are counted, and as `not shown` otherwise. A `not shown` stays
  `not shown`. A return that Part A's order cannot give, such as `not shown`
  beside a marker listed as shown, is read by these same rules and listed in the
  report. In the main reading the `with` total of a `produces` claim is the same
  whether carried-out markers are counted or not, because an outcome alone stays
  `with`. What changes is the split of `with`, the `against` codes, and the links
  that show the condition only.

Every result is computed under the four readings below, with each of these three
taken both ways: thirty-two combinations. The test plan names the combination
the pass bar is read on. Every other combination is reported beside it.

### Rows reported apart

- **`not found`.** A row that either coder marks `not found` counts as a
  disagreement on every field of that pair, even when both coders marked it. Each
  is checked against the paper and listed.
- **Marker sources.** A pair whose passage holds the sentence that one of the
  claim's markers came from can show that marker by construction. Results are
  reported with such pairs and without them.
- **Sides no marker can show.** Pairs on a side marked `none printed`, which only
  a name can show, and pairs on a `maps` claim with `no mapping given`, whose
  outcome is never shown, are reported apart.
- **Practices.** The report lists, claim by claim: the claims whose condition is
  a practice working with no `working` marker; the claims whose statement and
  printed direction word the condition differently; and, for each claim with
  tagged markers, the links whose condition rests only on `carried out` markers.
- **Named absences.** A condition coded `absent, named` keeps an outcome from
  counting only while names are counted. With names set aside, the pair becomes
  an outcome alone. On a tagged condition with a `carried out` marker listed, it
  becomes a condition with its outcome when carried-out markers are counted. So
  not counting names can add support as well as remove it. These pairs are
  listed.

### Why the table reads as it does

- **A tendency is tested where the condition holds.** A case with the condition
  and the outcome supports it; a case with the condition and not the outcome runs
  against it. A case without the condition says nothing about a tendency, whether
  the outcome came about (other things can bring it about) or not (a negative
  case fits, but does not test).
- **Necessity is tested where the outcome occurs.** A dish that left untasted runs
  against *a dish leaves only after the head chef tastes it*; a tasted dish that
  was sent back does not. So "both sides" on a `requires` claim is coded where the
  outcome occurred.
- **An outcome alone stays `with`** for `produces` and `requires`, as decided on
  27 September, flagged so it can be counted apart. For `maps` it is `illustrates`:
  naming a kind of outcome shows nothing about which condition sorts into it. A
  `with` records that the passage shows the outcome going the asserted way. It
  does not record that the passage shows the relation. So no count of `with` is
  reported without its split: condition and outcome shown, or outcome only.
- **One fact, one role** keeps an outcome from being counted a second time as its
  own condition, which is how a practice's "success" came to be read off its
  result.

### Four readings, all computed

Every result is computed under all four readings, in each combination of names,
patterns and practices above. The rule for the disputed cases is shown rather
than chosen in advance:

| reading | outcome only | negative case (`produces`) | outcome without condition (`produces`) | other cause (`produces`) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **main** (the table above; called "v1.1" in the last test) | `with` | `illustrates` | `illustrates` | `illustrates` |
| **strict** | `illustrates` | `illustrates` | `illustrates` | `illustrates` |
| **covariation** | `illustrates` | `with` | `against` | `illustrates` |
| **brief-v2 literal** (brief v2's box read word for word) | `with` | `against` | `against` | `against` |

Under the covariation reading, an outcome as asserted with another cause named is
still `illustrates`, as in the main reading. In the brief-v2 literal reading every
form except `describes` is read as `produces`. An outcome the other way is
`against` whatever the condition. An outcome as asserted is `against` if the
condition is absent or another cause is named.

Each reading gives a five-value code (`with`, `against`, `illustrates`,
`qualifies`, no link) and a three-value code (`with`, `against`, neither).

## Part C — left out of version 2.1, deliberately

- **Coding under a second set of markers, and a second passage session.** A
  second marker session writes its markers independently, and the two sets are
  compared, as the test plan describes. Coders use the first set only, so the
  test does not show how far the second set would change the links. The passages
  are written once. Both are read before the freeze, as Part B says, and the
  report prints them.
- **Separate markers for absence.** `absent` and `other way` are judged from the
  same markers. A passage must rule out every marker, or every `working` marker
  where they are tagged. Markers are sufficient signs, not a full definition, so
  some real absences will be coded `not shown`.
- **The bare name of a practice.** Where the condition is a practice working, a
  passage that only names the practice shows nothing of the condition. The name
  is no sign that the practice worked. Counting it as a sign that the practice
  was carried out would need a second mark from coders.
- **A failure described where no `working` marker is listed.** Where the paper
  gives no sign of a practice working apart from the outcome, nothing says what
  its working would look like. So a passage that describes the practice failing
  is not coded `absent`. It is coded by its `carried out` marker, if it shows
  one. A name for the practice failing is still `absent, named`.
- **The occasion as part of a case.** The same-case rule asks for the same people
  or thing, not the same occasion. A passage is one occurrence or one recurrence
  (Part P).
- **Finer kinds of support,** such as co-occurrence, sequence or an attributed
  cause. The flags on each code say what a passage contributes: outcome only,
  condition only, a negative case, another cause.
- **When a typology is one claim or several.** That belongs to the rules for
  extracting claims. The claims are taken as given here.
- **A claim map** and links derived through it. These rules never link a broad
  claim automatically, and the broad-and-narrow rule limits placement to the
  narrow claim.
- **Splitting a claim that asserts two relations.** The marker session codes the
  relation whose outcome is printed and flags the other.
- **A comparison of stub and passage.** Version 1.1's page re-check is dropped,
  because judgment 1 is now made from the passage.
- **Same-event marks and episode ids; a confidence scale.**

## Changes from version 2

Version 2 was frozen on 30 September and sent to no session. A colleague reviewed
it with the paper on 1 October. On 1–3 October five of her points were
adopted, the second in the form of tags, and the amended text was checked
independently twice. On 3 October the order in item 2 and the rule on reasons
in item 3 were confirmed, and the text was frozen.

1. **The absence examples.** Two examples coded `absent` where one marker's
   contrary left another marker open. They now rule every marker out, and new
   rows show a single contrary as `not shown`. The rule is unchanged.
2. **A practice carried out, and a practice working.** The condition is worded
   as the claim words it. Part S says what counts as a practice. Where the
   condition is a practice working or succeeding, the marker session lists both
   kinds of sign and tags each marker `working` or `carried out`. A `maps`
   claim's categories take no tags. Coders check every marker and name each one
   shown. They code the condition in a fixed order: a `working` marker shown is
   `present`; the condition ruled out is `absent`; a name for the whole
   condition holding, or for a state that rules it out, where nothing else shows
   which, is `present, named` or `absent, named`; a `carried out` marker shown
   is `present`. The practice's own name does not count. A condition that joins a
   practice working with something else follows the same order, and a name can
   complete a joint condition whose other part the passage shows. Part B computes
   every result with carried-out markers counted and without, and the report
   lists the claims with no `working` marker. Version 2 took "the practice
   itself, as the paper describes it being done" as the sign of a practice
   working. A practice failing is now shown by signs of it failing.
3. **Keep the mode.** An order shows the order, and a wish the wish. A fact a
   person gives as their reason counts as a reported fact; that it was the cause
   counts only under other cause. A reason that is the claim's own condition in
   the person's words is not another cause.
4. **The same case.** The condition and the outcome are coded for one case: the
   people or the thing the passage reports each of. Where a passage reports
   several cases, a fixed order says which one to code. Where the outcomes go
   different ways and nothing in the passage tells the cases apart, the outcome
   is `not shown`. A coder may read back before a passage only to find out who
   or which thing is meant. Nothing else said of them there counts.
5. **`none`, and `bounded`.** Exceptions do not show that there is no relation.
   When a treatment changes the condition or the outcome of a `bounded` pair,
   the pair gets no link or `illustrates`.
6. **Reporting.** No `with` count is reported without its split. With patterns as
   readings, a `none` claim can show nothing, by the treatment's doing. Pairs
   with a named absence are listed, because setting a name aside can add
   support. The reply format asks for the markers shown in a list apart from the
   reason.
7. **Part C** now names what was considered and left out: separate markers for
   absence, a practice's bare name, a failure described where no `working`
   marker is listed, the occasion as part of a case, finer kinds of support, and
   when a typology is one claim or several. A second marker session is run for
   comparison, but coders use the first set.
8. **The coders' table** prints each passage's text beside its first and last
   words. The paper governs where they differ.

Not adopted: the strict reading as the default for an outcome alone (the rule
of 27 September stands); another cause as a complement; and a claim's source
sentence counted as evidence.

## Changes from version 1.1, as made in version 2

Items 1, 3, 4, 6 and 7 carry out the decisions of 30 September: decisions 1 to
3, and the three made after the first review (1a, 2a and 3a).
Parts of items 1, 5 and 10 carry out the three made after the second review. The
others follow from these decisions, or from the two reviews.

1. **Markers** (decision 2). Each claim gets markers for its condition and for
   its outcome: as many as the paper gives separate signs, most often one to
   four; more than four were allowed after the second review. A separate session
   writes them from the paper's own definitions and descriptions, with the page
   and the opening words of each one's source sentence (Part S). They are fixed
   before any coder sees a pair. Version 1.1 deferred markers in its Part C.
2. **The claim specification moves to the marker session.** A marker is written
   for a condition, and a `maps` claim's markers depend on its form. So the marker
   session records each claim's condition and form, under version 1.1's rules,
   which move unchanged from Part A to Part S. Coders take them as given, and may
   note once where they think one is wrong. Form agreed at κ 1.00 on the last
   test, and this test does not measure it again.
3. **Judgment 1 from the passage** (decision 1). A separate session fixes each
   passage's first and last words before any coder sees a pair (Part P; decision
   3a). The passage is every sentence that reports the occurrence or the
   recurrence, and none of the comment before or after it. For the author's own
   statement, it is the sentences that make the statement. The stub only helps
   to find it. Judgment 1 is made for every pair before judgments 2 and 3, as in
   version 1.1, but with the paper from the start.
4. **What "shows" means** (decision 2). A passage shows a marker, in any
   words, with no further step: what it reports can be said in the marker's words
   without adding a fact, a cause, a motive or a meaning. One marker is enough, and
   part of a marker is not the marker. `none printed` means not shown. The
   "partly" rule is retired. The reason names the marker and quotes the words.
5. **What the passage reports, not what the author makes of it.** This follows
   from decision 1: a passage carries more of the author than a stub does.
   - The author's explanation never counts toward judgment 1. A fact the passage
     reports counts, even inside a "because" clause.
   - A thought or feeling the passage reports people having counts as reported,
     even when it quotes no one (decided after the second review).
   - Other cause is `yes` only for a reason the people gave, or for an order, a
     rule or an instruction the passage reports.
   - `absent` and `other way` mean that the passage rules the condition or the
     outcome out, usually by the contrary of a marker. The contrary of one marker
     is not enough while another could still hold.
6. **Names** (decision 1a). A passage that only names the condition or the
   outcome is marked `named`. A name is the claim's name for it, a word for it,
   another of the paper's abstract terms, or one of the author's categories
   applied to people or events. Words that describe an act anyone could see are
   never a name. A name for a contrary state is `absent, named` or `other way,
   named`, and a name that appears only in the author's explanation is set aside.
   Every result is computed with names counted and without.
7. **A third kind, `pattern`** (decision 3, and decision 2a). It holds the
   patterns across cases that version 1.1 counted as reports, including those in
   the author's own categories and sortings of the cases observed into types.
   Version 1.1's tie rule is dropped: a pattern the author also explains stays a
   `pattern`, as a single occurrence the author explains stays a `report`, and
   judgment 1 sets the explanation aside. A `reading` reports no occurrence and no
   recurrence among the cases observed. A standing practice is a `pattern`. A
   figure is a `pattern` only if it counts, lists or sorts the cases observed.
8. **`maps`.** The categories and each one's kind of outcome come from the
   specification, taken from the claim's statement and printed direction. `no
   mapping given` replaces the rule for a direction without a mapping. New rules
   cover a passage that shows several categories, and a paper that defines the
   kinds of outcome by the categories.
9. **Both sides** has a rule for `requires` claims, which are tested where the
   outcome occurred.
10. **Part B.**
    - The markers and the passages are checked before their freeze, as decided
      after the second review. Only clear breaches are fixed, each recorded.
    - The evidence link is computed with patterns as reports and as readings, and
      with names counted and not.
    - A row marked `not found` counts as a disagreement on every field.
    - Pairs on a marker's source sentence, and pairs on a side that can never be
      shown, are reported apart.
    - The reading called "v1.1" is renamed "main"; its table is unchanged.
11. **Dropped.**
    - The page re-check, because judgment 1 is already made from the page.
    - The check of each model's brief-v2 codes against its own observations,
      because the next test has no brief-v2 arm.

**Unchanged:**

- the four rules;
- the condition and form rules, though they have moved to Part S;
- "one fact, one role" and `bounded`, reworded for the passage;
- judgment 3 and the author link, word for word;
- the derivation table and its reasons.

**Not in this file:** the test plan (the sample, the bar and the combination it
is read on, the coders and their settings); the sender files and reply formats
for the marker session, the passage session and the coders; and the script. They
follow the freeze.
