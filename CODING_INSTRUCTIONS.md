# Coding instructions

*For this study's papers. Frozen 27 September 2026, before the first paper is
coded, and used unchanged for every paper. The changes from the earlier version
are listed at the end.*

You are coding one published qualitative research paper for a study of how
qualitative papers arrange their evidence. Someone has already read the paper
and recorded its evidence and its claims in a structured form. Your job is to
make a set of judgements about that material, independently, without seeing how
they were made the first time.

---

## Four stages, one workbook each

The judgements come in four stages. Each stage has its own workbook, and each
workbook comes back before the next is sent. Every stage asks for something that
a later workbook prints, so the order is what keeps your judgements independent
of each other.

| stage | section | you code |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 0 | the outcome of every claim |
| **A2** | 0b | the asserted direction of each claim that names an outcome |
| **V** | V | the voice of every fragment, from the paper |
| **B** | 1–7 | direction, core, stated as, gap kind, pair drawn by authors, resolution period, gloss relation |

This file describes all four stages. **Code only the stage whose workbook you
have.**

---

## What you have

| | |
|---|---|
| the paper | read it in full before coding anything |
| the workbook for this stage | the shaded columns are yours; each tab has one row per judgement |

Every field you are coding has been removed from the workbook. If you find one
filled in, stop and say so — it is a mistake, and it contaminates the row.

## Two rules before you start

**Do not ask what any of these codes is used for, and I will not tell you until
the coding is finished.** Some of them feed later steps, and knowing which answer
leads where is enough to tilt a borderline call without your noticing. This is
the whole reason for a second coder, so the silence is the point, not rudeness.

**Do not try to be consistent with the first coder.** You are not checking their
work and you are not trying to match it. Where we disagree, that disagreement is
the measurement — it tells us the definition is loose, which is something we need
to know and cannot find out any other way. A coder who guesses what the other
person probably said produces a number that means nothing.

Two smaller things. Work through one tab at a time rather than one row at a time
across tabs. And fill the reason column even when the answer feels obvious: when
we compare, the reasons are what tell us whether we disagreed about the paper or
about the definition.

---

## 0. Outcome — Stage A, sent on its own

**This tab comes in a separate workbook and is returned before you see the
others.** The reason is mechanical, not ceremonial: the direction tab prints each
claim's outcome for you to code against, so if you had both at once your outcome
codes would no longer be independent of what you saw there.

Each row gives a claim. **What does it say gets affected?**

Write the outcome as a short noun phrase — the thing that varies. *Time taken to
reach a decision. Conflict between two teams. Staff turnover.* Not a sentence,
not the claim restated.

**If the claim names nothing that varies, write `none`.** Plenty of claims
describe how something is rather than assert that one thing affects another, and
`none` is the right answer for those. Do not manufacture an outcome to fill the
cell.

**If a claim names something that varies but asserts no direction on it** — it
says the thing matters, not which way it goes — write `none` too, and say why in
the reason column.

Two claims can share an outcome. That is fine and expected.

---

## 0b. Asserted direction — Stage A2, sent on its own

**This tab also comes in a separate workbook.** It is sent after Stage A comes
back, for the same reason: the Stage B workbook prints these answers, so you
could not make them independently once you had seen it.

Only the claims that name an outcome appear here. A claim that affects nothing
has no direction to assert.

Each row gives a claim and **the outcome recorded for it** — which may not be the
outcome you wrote in Stage A. Use the one printed here. You are not being asked
to agree with it; you are being asked, *given that this is the outcome, which way
does the claim say it goes?*

Write the direction as a short conditional. *The more X, the harder Y. More A
leads to more B. When Z is present, W becomes possible.* The shape that matters
is: what varies, and which way the claim pushes the outcome when it does.

Two things to watch.

**Sign.** Say plainly whether the outcome goes up or down. "Affects the time
taken" is not a direction; "the more people must sign off, the longer the
decision takes" is.

**Match the printed outcome's polarity.** If the outcome is phrased as a
difficulty and the claim makes things easier, say so — *less difficulty* — rather
than silently rewriting the outcome as ease. Changing the outcome's sign halfway
through makes the pair unreadable.

If a claim names an outcome but genuinely asserts no direction on it, write
`none` and say why.

## V. Voice — Stage V, sent on its own

**This tab comes in its own workbook too**, after Stage A2 and before Stage B.
Stage B's workbook prints a summary of each fragment, and the way a summary is
worded can tip a voice judgement, so voice is coded first, from the page.

Is this fragment the paper **reporting**, or the author **interpreting**?

| code | means |
|---|---|
| `reported` | the paper shows you something that happened, was said, or was the case |
| `author_insight` | the author's own interpretation of what was going on |

**Code this one from the paper, not from the description in the row.** Every
row gives a page reference and a short description of the fragment, to help you
find the sentence. Go to the page, find the sentence, and code what the author
wrote.

This is the opposite of the rule for direction, and the reversal is deliberate.
Direction is coded from the stub so that no surrounding context can leak into the
judgement. Voice is a fact about the original sentence, and a stub cannot carry
it reliably: summarising in fresh words tends to convert "for these workers, X
was a way of showing Y" into "X showed Y", which reads as a report whatever the
source was. If you code voice from stubs you will code interpretation as report
and never know.

**The test.** If the sentence is the author's own words rather than a report of
what someone did or said, ask: can you point to the single reported event this
sentence characterises?

- If yes, it is a summary of that one event and belongs with it. Code
  `reported`. This holds even when the sentence also explains why that event
  went as it did, or says what it meant to the people in it: that part is the
  event's gloss.
- If no — it interprets across several events, or explains why something
  happened, or states what something meant — code `author_insight`.

Most fragments will be `reported`. An author's explanation of why people behaved
as they did, across several events, is the clearest case of `author_insight`. So
is a sentence saying what an object meant or was worth to people in general. A
table or figure summarising the author's framework is usually `author_insight`
too.

**Where the description and the page disagree, the page wins, and say so in the
reason column.** That mismatch is worth more to us than the code is.

Where a sentence reports the reasoning of people in the setting across several
events, in the author's own voice and without quoting anyone, it is genuinely
ambiguous. Code your best judgement and flag it. About one event, it is
`reported`, as above.

## Stage B — sections 1 to 7

Stage B comes last, in one workbook with a tab for each section below.

## 1. Direction

> **Direction in two steps. Read the claim's outcome first.**
>
> 1. **Does the stub report anything about the outcome itself?** If it reports
>    the setting, or the conditions, or the general phenomenon, but says nothing
>    about the outcome itself → `illustrates`, however well the fragment fits the
>    claim's subject matter. If this step is genuinely unclear, write the
>    tie-breaker below into the reason column and code from it.
> 2. **If it does, which way did the outcome go?** The way the asserted
>    direction says → `with`. The opposite way, **or** the outcome occurred
>    without the condition the claim names as its cause → `against`. The stub
>    explicitly bounds or limits the claim → `qualifies`. The stub need not show
>    the condition: an outcome going the asserted way, with nothing said about
>    why, → `with`.
>
> Keep the outcome's polarity as it is; do not re-sign it.

Each row pairs one **fragment** — a piece of what the paper reports happened, was
said, or was the case — with one **claim** the paper makes.

Every claim names an **outcome**: the thing it says gets affected. It is printed
in its own column, beside the claim. Some claims have no outcome and that column
is blank; for those, `with` and `against` are not available at all, so code
`illustrates` or `qualifies`.

**The test, in order.**

**Step 1. Does the stub report anything about that claim's outcome?**

If no — if the stub reports the setting, or the conditions, or the general
phenomenon, but says nothing about the outcome itself — the code is
`illustrates`. However well the fragment fits the claim's subject matter. This
is the step that does most of the work, and most fragments stop here.

**Step 2. If yes, which way did the outcome go?**

| code | when |
|---|---|
| `with` | the outcome went the way the claim's asserted direction says |
| `against` | **either** the outcome went the opposite way, **or** the outcome occurred without the condition the claim names as its cause |
| `qualifies` | the stub explicitly bounds or limits the claim |

The second half of `against` is easy to miss and it matters. A claim saying
*time pressure makes cooks skip the tasting step* is run against by a stub
showing a cook skipping the tasting step on a slow afternoon with no orders
waiting. The outcome is present, and the stub shows the named cause absent. It
is run against, too, by a stub in which a cook skips the step because the recipe
card has gone missing: the stub puts the outcome down to another cause.

**An outcome without its cause.** Had that stub said nothing about how busy the
kitchen was, or about why the step was skipped, the code would be `with`: the
outcome went the asserted way, and nothing in the stub shows it happening
without the cause. The second half of `against` needs a stub that shows the
condition absent, or that puts the outcome down to another cause and not to the
condition. An absence you would have to infer does not count.

**When step 1 is genuinely unclear**, write this sentence in the reason column
and code from it: *if this claim were false, this stub would instead say ___.*
If you cannot complete it, or it comes out the same as what the stub already
says, the answer is `illustrates`.

**Code from the stub, not from the paper.** The stub is the summary in the sheet.
This is deliberate and it is the opposite of the rule for voice above. If the
stub does not carry what you need, that is itself a finding: code what the stub
supports and say so.

**Write the reason before you choose the code.** It is easy to pick a direction
and then justify it; the reverse is harder and more honest.

**Your reason may cite only what the stub says.** Not the neighbouring fragment,
not the surrounding paragraph, not what you know about how organisations work,
not an inference. A reason that needs a different fragment to make sense does not
stand, and the code it supports does not stand either.

`against` is narrow. It does not mean "this is interesting" or "this also points
somewhere else". Most fragments will not be `against`, and that is expected.

## 2. Core

Does this claim carry the paper's contribution?

`true` if the paper would lose its argument without it. `false` if it is
scaffolding, background, or a step on the way to something else.

A paper usually has a handful of core claims, not one and not all of them. Do not
aim for any particular number.

---

## 3. Stated as

How firmly does the paper put this claim?

| code | means |
|---|---|
| `established` | asserted as something the paper has shown |
| `ventured` | hedged, offered, proposed, suggested as worth considering |

Judge the paper's language, not whether you find the claim convincing. Watch for
hedging that is only stylistic modesty, and for confident phrasing around a claim
the evidence does not really reach — both directions of mismatch occur, and you
are coding the phrasing. Where a claim is stated in more than one place, code its
firmest statement, and note the hedged places in the reason column.

---

## 4. Gap kind

Each row is a limit the paper states **about itself**. Which kind is it?

| code | means |
|---|---|
| `scope_limit` | the paper says what it does not cover |
| `absent_evidence` | the paper says it lacks some evidence |
| `unresolved_question` | the paper names a question it leaves open |
| `acknowledged_ambiguity` | the paper says something is unclear or could be read two ways |
| `vocabulary_mismatch` | the paper says a term is used differently elsewhere |

Some will sit between two. Pick one, and use the reason column to say which other
one you considered and why you rejected it. Those notes matter more here than
anywhere else, because this tab has only a few rows.

---

## 5. Pair drawn by authors

Each row is two episodes set against each other. Did **the paper** draw that
comparison, or has the analyst assembled it?

`true` if the paper itself puts the two side by side and treats the contrast as
meaningful. `false` if the two episodes both appear but the paper never compares
them.

The test is whether the comparison is on the page, not whether it is a reasonable
comparison to make.

---

## 6. Resolution period

The paper's story has been divided into ordered periods. In which one does the
story close — the point after which the paper treats the matter as settled?

Where the list of periods starts at P0, P0 holds events the paper places before
its story begins.

If you think the paper does not close, say so in the reason column rather than
forcing a period.

---

## 7. Gloss relation

Each row shows a fragment and, beside it, the author's own summary of that same
fragment. How does the summary sit against what the fragment reports?

| code | means |
|---|---|
| `consistent` | the summary says what the fragment supports, no more |
| `goes_beyond` | the summary adds something the fragment does not carry, without contradicting it |
| `diverges` | the summary and the fragment point different ways |
| `none` | no relation worth recording |

The distinction between `goes_beyond` and `diverges` is the one that matters.
Ask: could both be true at once? If yes, it is `goes_beyond`. If believing the
summary means disbelieving part of the fragment, it is `diverges`.

Read the surrounding fragments from the same episode, not only the one shown:
the relation may be to one of them, especially where one event has ended up in
more than one fragment.

If the gloss has more than one part, code the part that departs furthest from
what the fragment reports (`diverges` before `goes_beyond` before `consistent`),
and name that part in the reason.

---

## When you are unsure

Code your best judgement and say what made it hard. Never leave a cell blank,
and never invent a code that is not on the list.

If you find yourself wanting a code that does not exist, write the one you would
have used in the reason column. That is useful — it usually means the scheme is
missing something.

## When you are done

Send the workbook back before any of it is discussed. Once we have talked, the
agreement figure is about the conversation rather than about the coding, and it
cannot be recovered.

**Nothing is discussed until every paper in the study has been coded.** Talking
over one paper's disagreements would show you how the first coder reads before
you code the next one. After the last paper, we go through every disagreement
together, and I will answer any question about what the codes are for.
